Claims Leakage Detection AI: Why Most Insurers Are Still Losing Millions Despite 'Cutting-Edge' Tools

Most AI-powered claims leakage detection systems fail to address the real gaps—here’s how insurers can fix it before 2027.
May 30, 2026

The average P&C insurer loses 5-10% of its claims payouts to leakage—not fraud, not catastrophic events, but preventable errors, inefficiencies, and undetected overpayments. That’s $30-$60 billion annually in the U.S. alone, according to Deloitte’s 2025 Insurance Fraud and Leakage Benchmarking Study. Yet despite the hype around AI-driven detection, most insurers still rely on rules-based systems that flag only the most obvious cases, leaving the rest buried in the noise.

The problem isn’t a lack of data or even a lack of AI—it’s that most solutions are solving for the wrong problem. They’re optimized for fraud detection (which accounts for ~10% of leakage) while ignoring the other 90%: misclassified claims, incorrect reserve settings, subrogation failures, and vendor overbilling. Here’s why the current approach is broken—and what actually works.

The Three Blind Spots in AI-Powered Leakage Detection#

1. The Fraud Obsession Distraction#

Fraud is sexy. It’s the stuff of headlines—staged accidents, fake injuries, elaborate scams. Insurers love to tout their AI fraud detection models, and vendors like Shift Technology and FRISS have built entire businesses around it. But here’s the dirty secret: fraud represents just 1-2% of total claims costs, per McKinsey’s 2024 Global Insurance Pools report. The other 8-9% of leakage? It’s hiding in plain sight.

Take Lemonade’s early experiments with AI claims triage. Their model was designed to detect fraudulent behavior (e.g., inconsistent injury reports, suspicious timing) and achieved a 75% reduction in fraudulent payouts. Impressive, but it barely moved the needle on overall leakage. Why? Because Lemonade’s real leakage problem wasn’t fraud—it was overpayments on legitimate claims due to misaligned incentives between adjusters and vendors. The AI was looking for the wrong signals.

2. The Black Box Problem No One Wants to Talk About#

Most AI leakage detection models operate as “black boxes.” They spit out a risk score or a flag, but adjusters have no idea why a claim was flagged. This isn’t just an academic concern—it’s a practical one. A 2025 Accenture survey of 500 insurance claims leaders found that 68% of adjusters ignore AI-generated flags when they can’t understand the rationale behind them.

AXA’s experience with Tractable’s AI for auto claims illustrates this. Tractable’s computer vision model assesses vehicle damage and estimates repair costs with 90%+ accuracy. But when AXA rolled it out in France, adjusters pushed back. They didn’t trust the model’s estimates because they couldn’t see the underlying logic (e.g., why a dent was classified as a $500 repair vs. $1,200). AXA had to retrofit the system with explainability layers—adding heatmaps and confidence scores—to get adjuster buy-in. The lesson? AI that can’t explain itself is AI that won’t be used.

3. The Data Silo Trap#

Claims leakage doesn’t happen in a vacuum. It’s the result of broken processes across underwriting, claims, and vendor management. Yet most AI tools are deployed in isolation. For example:

  • A claims adjuster might flag a suspicious water damage claim, but if the underwriting team doesn’t know the property’s prior claims history, they can’t contextualize the risk.
  • A vendor might overbill for a repair, but if the insurer’s AI only looks at claims data (not vendor performance history), it won’t catch the pattern.

Allianz’s 2025 pilot with an AI leakage detection platform revealed this gap. The tool flagged $12 million in potential leakage across 50,000 claims—but 40% of those flags were false positives because the model didn’t have access to underwriting or vendor data. The fix? Allianz had to integrate its claims, policy, and vendor management systems into a unified data lake before the AI could deliver real value.

What Actually Works: The 2026 Playbook#

So how do insurers move beyond the hype and build AI leakage detection that actually stops the bleeding? Here’s the framework that separates the leaders from the laggards.

1. Start with the Leakage, Not the AI#

Most insurers begin with the technology (“We need AI!”) and then look for problems to solve. The smarter approach? Start with the leakage itself. Map out your claims lifecycle and identify where overpayments are happening. Is it:

  • Reserve setting? (e.g., adjusters lowballing reserves to hit cycle time targets)
  • Vendor overbilling? (e.g., body shops charging for unnecessary repairs)
  • Subrogation failures? (e.g., missed opportunities to recover from at-fault parties)

Root Insurance took this approach in 2024. Instead of deploying a generic AI fraud tool, they built a custom model to detect vendor overbilling in auto glass repairs. The result? A 15% reduction in glass repair payouts within six months. The key was focusing on a specific, high-impact leakage source—not chasing the AI flavor of the month.

2. Augment, Don’t Replace, Human Adjusters#

AI should be a co-pilot, not a replacement. The best systems don’t just flag claims—they provide adjusters with actionable insights. For example:

  • Instead of saying, “This claim is high-risk,” the AI should say, “This claim has a 78% chance of overpayment because the repair estimate exceeds the 90th percentile for similar vehicles in this region. Here’s the comparable data.”
  • Instead of flagging a claim for fraud, the AI should highlight inconsistencies (e.g., “The claimant’s injury report mentions a back injury, but their social media shows them lifting weights three days after the accident”).

This is the approach Chubb took with its “AI Adjuster Assistant” in 2025. The tool doesn’t make decisions—it surfaces relevant data (e.g., prior claims, weather reports, vehicle history) and lets adjusters connect the dots. The result? A 22% reduction in leakage on flagged claims, with no increase in adjuster workload.

3. Measure What Matters: Leakage Reduction, Not Flag Rates#

Most insurers measure AI leakage detection success by the number of flags generated. That’s a vanity metric. The real KPI? Actual leakage reduction. For example:

  • If your AI flags 1,000 claims but only 10% of those flags result in recovered overpayments, your model is noisy.
  • If your AI flags 100 claims but recovers $5 million in overpayments, it’s effective.

Liberty Mutual’s 2025 leakage detection program took this to heart. They stopped reporting flag rates to leadership and instead tracked “leakage dollars recovered per adjuster hour.” This forced the AI team to focus on precision over volume—and it paid off. Their leakage recovery rate doubled in 12 months.

4. The Ethical Elephant in the Room#

AI leakage detection isn’t just a technical challenge—it’s an ethical one. Models trained on historical claims data can perpetuate biases. For example:

  • If past adjusters were more likely to deny claims from certain ZIP codes, the AI might learn to flag those claims more aggressively.
  • If vendor overbilling was historically overlooked in affluent neighborhoods, the AI might miss those cases.

State Farm’s 2025 audit of its AI leakage detection model revealed this risk. Their model was 20% more likely to flag claims from low-income neighborhoods for fraud—even when controlling for other variables. The fix? They retrained the model on a balanced dataset and added bias-detection checks. The lesson? AI leakage detection requires guardrails, not just algorithms.

How to Evaluate a Claims Leakage AI Vendor in 2026#

Not all AI leakage detection tools are created equal. Here’s how to separate the wheat from the chaff when evaluating vendors:

  1. Ask for leakage-specific case studies, not fraud detection demos.

    • Bad: “Our AI reduces fraud by 50%.” (Who cares? Fraud is 10% of the problem.)
    • Good: “Our AI identified $8 million in vendor overbilling for a regional insurer in 2025, with a 92% precision rate.”

  2. Demand explainability.

    • If the vendor can’t show you why a claim was flagged, walk away. Look for tools that provide:
      • Confidence scores
      • Comparable claims data
      • Visual explanations (e.g., heatmaps for damage assessment)

  3. Test for data integration.

    • Ask: “Does your tool integrate with underwriting, claims, and vendor data?” If the answer is no, it’s not a leakage detection tool—it’s a fraud detection tool.

  4. Run a pilot with real adjuster feedback.

    • Don’t trust vendor demos. Deploy the tool in a live environment and track:
      • Adjuster adoption rate (Are they actually using it?)
      • Leakage dollars recovered (Not just flags generated)
      • False positive rate (How much time are adjusters wasting on bad flags?)

Bottom Line#

Claims leakage detection AI isn’t a silver bullet—it’s a scalpel. The insurers winning the leakage war in 2026 aren’t the ones with the fanciest AI; they’re the ones who:

  1. Focus on the 90% of leakage that’s not fraud.
  2. Build tools that augment, not replace, human adjusters.
  3. Measure success in dollars recovered, not flags generated.
  4. Treat AI as a process improvement tool, not a standalone solution.

The technology is here. The data is here. The question is whether insurers will use it to plug the real leaks—or just keep bailing water with a thimble.
