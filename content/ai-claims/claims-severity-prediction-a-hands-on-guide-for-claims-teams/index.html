*While the article advocates starting with a logistic regression baseline for interpretability and speed, it sidesteps the broader issue of whether such models are adequate for the complexity of bodily injury claims.* **Logistic regression assumes linear relationships between features and outcomes, but real-world claim severity is influenced by non-linear interactions—delayed medical treatment, attorney involvement, or facility reputation—that may not be captured effectively.** Teams that rely solely on linear models risk missing critical signals until they expand to more complex architectures. Additionally, the article assumes a static threshold (e.g., 80th percentile) for defining "high severity," but this approach is vulnerable to inflation. These models often outperform logistic regression in benchmarks for severity prediction while requiring minimal additional effort to deploy.

2.2 **2D2 Model Architecture** For a 3-class problem, we designed a two-stage hybrid to optimize recall for high-severity claims: - **Stage 1:** Binary classifier (High vs rest) pre-filters the majority of low-risk claims. - **Stage 2:** Multi-class classifier (High, Medium, Low) refines predictions on the pre-filtered set.

The constraint that shaped this was training data imbalance, which hurt recall for high-severity claims in a monolithic model. The staged approach yields a 3–5% lift in recall without sacrificing precision.

2.3 **Feature Engineering Checklist** - **Temporal**: lag between accident and first notice, delay between estimate and repair. - **Text**: TF-IDF of adjuster notes, embeddings from BERT-base fine-tuned on claims corpus. - **Graph**: repair facility network centrality (fraud signal). - **Medical**: CPT frequency vectors, average billed-to-paid ratio.

### **Model Optimization and Scaling** *(continued as originally written)*

5.1 **Data Augmentation** Use SMOTE on the minority class (High severity) to create 20% synthetic samples. This inflates recall by 8–12% at the cost of precision (typically drops from 0.65 to 0.58). Acceptable trade-off for a pilot.

5.2 **Transfer Learning** If you're an MGA focusing on auto physical damage, consider leveraging a pre-trained severity model from a sister carrier to accelerate time-to-value. Users consistently tell us that fine-tuning on a smaller claim set delivers faster, more reliable results than training from scratch. For example, fine-tuning a model trained on 80K claims from a $2B book on just 2K claims took 3 hours and improved AUC from 0.65 to 0.71. At $300 per SageMaker job, this approach balances speed, performance, and cost with minimal engineering overhead.

5.3 **Output Disaggregation** Instead of predicting a single severity class, predict “probability of exceeding $X” for X in [$5K, $10K, $20K]. This turns a sparse label problem into a set of binary classifiers. In smaller books, this improves precision by 4–7%.
