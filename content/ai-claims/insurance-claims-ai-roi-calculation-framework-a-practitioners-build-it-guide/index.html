When predominantly Black neighborhoods have a history of denied claims, undervalued properties, and limited contractor networks, the AI doesn’t just reflect those patterns—it accelerates them. This isn’t just a policy failure; it’s a systems failure. The system responds by reinforcing the very inequities it should mitigate.

Without these interventions, the system will continue to respond by perpetuating the very disparities it was designed to address.

### 1. Define the ROI Boundary: What Counts as “Claim”? Most pilots fail because the ROI boundary is arbitrary. A vendor’s slide deck might claim “30% cycle-time reduction,” but if the model only handles 5% of your claim volume, the net effect on loss ratio is a rounding error. Start with the FNOL volume that actually hits your adjuster queue. For a mid-tier P&C carrier, first-notice-of-loss (FNOL) funnel typically breaks down like this: Automotive : Bodily injury (BI) vs. property damage (PD) split. BI claims require medical records and often bodily injury adjusters—AI struggles here. PD claims (glass, hail, fender benders) are the sweet spot. Property : Catastrophe vs. non-cat. Parametric triggers work for wind/hail, but adjusters still walk every roof after a CAT 3+. AI ROI for CAT claims is usually negative. Liability : Slip-and-fall, dog bites. These claims have high fraud risk and low average severity; AI pre-screening rarely pays off. Actionable rule: Exclude BI, CAT, and liability from your AI ROI model unless you have a proven fraud detection model trained on your book. Only include PD auto and small commercial property claims.

You'll find that focusing AI on low-complexity claims often leaves the most vulnerable policyholders in the lurch. Communities already hit hardest by severe weather or systemic healthcare gaps end up tangled in adversarial processes because their cases are too complex for automated systems.

Here's what I tell my team: when we automate only the easy claims, we're essentially telling families dealing with life-altering injuries or destroyed homes that their suffering doesn’t matter as much as efficiency. Algorithms can’t recognize human suffering, and that’s where the real harm happens.

The key insight is this: high-stakes claims deserve human compassion and nuanced judgment, especially when infrastructure and healthcare disparities are already stacking the deck against these communities.

### 2. Build the Baseline: Pre-AI Claims Economics You need two baselines: claims payout velocity and adjuster cost per claim . Model 1: Payout velocity. Pull three years of paid claims data and calculate the time-to-pay distribution by line of business. This isn’t just an average—you need the 50th, 75th, and 95th percentiles. Why? Because AI vendors often quote cycle-time reduction on the mean, masking the tail risk of delayed payouts that trigger regulatory complaints. Model 2: Adjuster cost per claim. For each closed claim, divide fully-loaded adjuster hours by total claims count. Include: salary, benefits, desk time, travel, and supervision overhead. A 2023 Conning & Co. analysis of 12 regional carriers found the average auto adjuster cost was $187 per claim, but small commercial property adjusters averaged $243 due to site visits. [Conning & Co., Property-Casualty Insurance Market Outlook 2023]

**Consumer Impact:** Baseline data often reflects historical disparities in claims handling. For example, a 2024 Urban Institute study found that in majority-Black neighborhoods, claims take on average 3.2 days longer to resolve than in white neighborhoods—even for identical damage. [Urban Institute, "Insurance Redlining 2.0," 2024] If AI models are trained on this skewed data without correction, they will perpetuate and automate these delays. Moreover, the focus on adjuster cost savings ignores the human cost of deprioritizing claims from communities that already face systemic delays in repairs and reimbursements. When AI accelerates payouts for high-volume, low-complexity claims in affluent areas while delaying or denying claims in marginalized communities, the combined ratio may improve—but at the expense of fairness and trust.

We chose a two-axis scoring system—technical feasibility and ROI velocity—to quickly filter use cases before investing in model training. The design principle was to avoid projects that either had too much technical risk (low feasibility) or took too long to show measurable impact (low ROI velocity).

When scoring feasibility, the constraint that shaped this was the availability and quality of labeled data. For PD damage severity estimation, 10,000+ labeled images and repair cost histories were non-negotiable. Without that, we couldn’t train a model with enough precision to reduce desk reviews meaningfully.

ROI velocity drove us toward use cases with clear, short-term benefits. Auto glass claim first notice auto-accept was a clear winner—it delivers ROI in 1–3 months. But it came with a trade-off: the highest ROI velocity use cases often disrupted revenue models for partners. We saw two pilots shut down because the MGA’s commission structure wasn’t aligned with AI-driven auto-accepts. The lesson was clear: technical feasibility and ROI matter, but so does ecosystem alignment.
