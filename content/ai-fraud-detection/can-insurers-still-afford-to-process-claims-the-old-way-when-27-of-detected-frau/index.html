The adjuster’s nightmare: too many flags, not enough signal At a mid-sized P&C carrier, real-time AI cut time to triage from 23 hours to 3 minutes. It also flooded the fraud unit with 400 alerts per day—95% false positives. Adjuster morale tanked; the system was shelved within six months. The failure wasn’t the model. It was the signal-to-noise ratio. The carrier had tuned the model for recall, not precision. It prioritized catching every possible fraud case, even at the cost of overwhelming analysts. How to tune for precision without losing recall Start with a precision target: aim for 70–75% of high-risk cases to be legitimate fraud or suspicious. Anything lower burns out teams and erodes trust. **Layer 2: Anomaly scoring – The illusion of outlier detection** Here’s the uncomfortable truth: most fraud detection systems treat unsupervised models like Isolation Forests and Autoencoders as magical silver bullets. They flag 15–20% of "missed" cases, but what if those outliers aren’t fraud at all? What if they’re just rare but legitimate transactions that the system is too rigid to understand? Most people miss this: anomaly detection isn’t just about spotting fraud—it’s about spotting *everything that doesn’t fit*, and then wasting months sorting the real fraud from the noise. **Layer 3: Expert-in-the-loop – When adjusters derail your model** The conventional wisdom is wrong here. The idea that adjuster overrides should automatically reduce flag weights assumes adjusters are infallible arbiters of fraud. But what if some adjusters are just lazy, overworked, or worse—colluding with fraudsters? One carrier saw precision improve in the first 90 days, but did they ever audit *why* those overrides happened? Maybe the model was right all along, and the adjusters were wrong. **Layer 4: Threshold decay – The false promise of dynamic thresholds** Dynamic thresholds sound smart, don’t they? Adjust escalation scores based on claim volume—higher during hurricanes, lower during quiet times. But here’s the catch: fraudsters adapt. They know when thresholds are low and exploit the gaps. That same carrier reduced false positives from 48% to 21% not because of smarter AI, but because they finally admitted their static thresholds were *too aggressive* in the first place. The real win wasn’t "smarter AI orchestration"—it was fixing a broken baseline. **The data problem: Garbage in, garbage out, but faster** Most carriers discover the hard way that real-time AI fraud analytics only works if the data feeding it is clean and current. But what if the real problem isn’t the data—it’s the assumption that fraud can be detected at all with current models? Maybe the issue isn’t "bad data," but that fraud is inherently probabilistic, and no amount of speed will fix that. **Common failure modes – Where models quietly fail** - **Stale policy data:** A lapsed policy still scoring claims? Sounds like a system that’s too brittle to handle real-world chaos. False negatives? More like a system that’s *too predictable*—fraudsters already know which gaps to exploit. - **Thresholds that never adapt:** Fixed escalation scores? That’s not precision—it’s rigid dogma. Fraud evolves; should our defenses stay static?

Incomplete telematics: A claimant’s telematics device stopped transmitting 48 hours before the accident. The model assumes normal driving, missing erratic behavior. Third-party feeds: Credit reports from Experian are 7 days old. The model scores based on outdated financial stress indicators.

Solutions: Event sourcing: Rebuild policy data as a stream of events (policy issued, endorsement added, cancellation) rather than a static snapshot. This keeps the model current.

Data contracts: Require vendors to deliver data in real time with SLAs. One carrier penalized a telematics provider $50K for each missed heartbeat. Data quality scoring: Assign a “data freshness score” to each feed. If a feed’s score drops below 80%, the model automatically downgrades its weight in scoring.

Meanwhile, the **Fairness** requirement forces re-weighting of socio-economic factors—adjusters can’t just rely on ZIP codes or unemployment rates, so the model’s sensitivity to other signals (like behavioral patterns) must improve. The system responds by tightening feedback loops between underwriters and data scientists, as old heuristics break down. The ripple effect extends further: carriers that struggle with **data readiness** face a compounding disadvantage. If a company can’t ingest real-time feeds from claims, telematics, and third-party sources, its models degrade, leading to poorer fraud detection. The system responds by widening the gap between leaders and laggards—leaders see leakage reduction and adjuster productivity gains, while laggards double down on manual reviews, increasing their operational costs. Even governance becomes a feedback mechanism: if adjusters can’t explain model decisions, regulators intervene, forcing carriers to invest in model interpretability tooling. And for CFOs demanding ROI in 12–18 months? The system responds by exposing the hard truth—real-time AI isn’t a quick fix but a long-term platform play. Those who treat it as such will align their data, people, and processes in a virtuous cycle, while others remain stuck in a feedback loop of inefficiency.
