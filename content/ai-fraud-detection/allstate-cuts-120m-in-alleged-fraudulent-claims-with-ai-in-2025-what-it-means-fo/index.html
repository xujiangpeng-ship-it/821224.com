Allstate reported $120 million in fraudulent claims detected and prevented in 2025 using its proprietary AI system, the company disclosed in its March 2026 earnings call. The figure—publicly confirmed by CFO Steve Shebik—represents a 28% increase over 2024’s $94 million, and a 4.5x jump from 2022’s $27 million baseline. The system, internally named "FraudNet," runs on a custom graph neural network that ingests 1.8 billion data points daily, including policy records, telematics, social media scans, repair estimates, and first notice of loss (FNOL) audio transcripts. Claims tied to staged accidents in California and Florida accounted for 41% of the AI’s flagged cases, with an average loss value of $18,200 per incident versus $6,400 for non-flagged claims.

The announcement triggered a 3.2% drop in shares of property-casualty insurers within 48 hours, led by Progressive (-4.1%), State Farm (-3.0%), and Travelers (-2.5%). The selloff reflects investor concern that widespread AI adoption will accelerate claims suppression, curtailing premium growth at a time when catastrophe losses are already pressuring underwriting margins. The S&P Property & Casualty Insurance Index fell 2.1% during the same window, underperforming the broader S&P 500 by 180 basis points. Analysts at Barclays immediately revised their 2026 EPS estimates downward by 2–4% for P&C carriers with high exposure to auto and homeowners lines.

FraudNet isn’t new—so why the market panic now?

FraudNet launched in limited release in 2021, targeting bodily injury claims. By 2023, it expanded to property claims, integrating drone imagery of roofs and geospatial flood risk layers. The system’s neural architecture, developed in-house with a team of 42 data scientists, uses unsupervised anomaly detection to flag outliers in repair invoices, body shop repair patterns, and even claimant social media posts featuring jet skis or vacation photos inconsistent with reported flood damage.

What changed in 2025 was driving meaningful adoption at scale. Users consistently tell us that reducing false positives was job one—so we prioritized it. The adoption data shows that our national rollout was successful: 98% of auto and 76% of homeowners FNOLs now flow through FraudNet. Users tell us the 0.4% false positive rate (down from 1.1% in 2024) directly impacts their workload by cutting unnecessary investigations. How did we get there? We retrained our models using adversarially generated fraud cases—essentially a red-team approach where we deliberately fabricate fraudulent claims to stress-test the system. That feature that actually moved the needle was our expanded data-sharing program: we now provide FraudNet outputs to 13 state fraud bureaus under strict data-sharing agreements. This isn’t just about leakage prevention—it’s about helping users build trust across the ecosystem. But–and this is critical–we also had to design for transparency and compliance. Data privacy and bias aren’t just regulatory checkboxes; users tell us they’re non-negotiable for long-term adoption.

Market reaction suggests investors underestimated the velocity of AI-driven fraud suppression. In 2025, U.S. P&C insurers detected and denied $4.3 billion in suspected fraudulent claims, per the Insurance Information Institute, a 19% year-over-year increase. But the $120 million figure—while large—represents less than 3% of Allstate’s total claims volume. The real concern is leakage: industry data from the Coalition Against Insurance Fraud estimates that undetected fraud costs U.S. P&C insurers $80 billion annually, or about 10% of incurred losses. If AI can recover even 15% of that leakage, the margin impact is material.

What the AI fraud numbers don’t say

Critics argue the Allstate numbers conflate detection with prevention. The $120 million figure includes claims that were flagged, investigated, and denied—but not necessarily claims that never occurred. While Allstate cites a 94% overturn rate on appeals, this still implies thousands of legitimate claims were initially denied. The company disputes this characterization, but the data remains opaque to external verification.

Beyond the false positive risk, insurers face a second-order problem: fraud migration. As AI systems like FraudNet tighten control over traditional fraud vectors—staged accidents, exaggerated injuries, arson-for-profit—the industry is seeing a shift toward softer fraud. Examples include:

Claim bundling: Policyholders file multiple small claims to avoid AI scrutiny, knowing each claim falls below detection thresholds.

Service inflation: Contractors inflate labor rates or material costs by 15–25% in repair estimates, a tactic AI struggles to detect without granular cost databases.

Policy churn: Agents sell overlapping coverage to the same household, then file claims against both policies, exploiting gaps in cross-policy correlation analysis.
