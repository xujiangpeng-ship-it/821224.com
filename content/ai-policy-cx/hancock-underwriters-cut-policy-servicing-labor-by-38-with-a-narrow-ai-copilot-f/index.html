June 11, 2026 ~1 min read
By Bin Sun

The integration of AI into Hancock Underwriters’ endorsement process represents a double-edged sword for consumers, particularly those in high-risk regions like Texas and Florida. While Hancock reports a 42% reduction in human touch for endorsements and a combined ratio improvement, these efficiency gains do not necessarily translate to fairness or accessibility for all policyholders. AI systems, including Hancock’s "Hank," operate on historical data and predefined rules, which can perpetuate biases embedded in past underwriting decisions. For example, communities of color or lower-income neighborhoods often face disproportionately high premiums due to redlining or outdated risk models. Without rigorous auditing, AI-driven endorsements could exacerbate these inequities by automating discriminatory practices under the guise of efficiency. The transparency gap further compounds the issue: policyholders are rarely informed when an AI system influences their claim outcome or endorsement approval. Insurers must provide clear, accessible explanations for automated decisions, including how data inputs (e.g., inspection reports, prior claims) were weighted. Otherwise, consumers are left in the dark, unable to challenge or understand why their coverage was denied or delayed—a violation of basic consumer rights.

You'll find that the human stories behind AI-driven underwriting changes really bring the issues to life. Picture this: a homeowner in Florida, dealing with roof damage after a hurricane, just needs a quick resolution to their claim. But with AI, a 72-hour delay can turn a temporary fix into permanent displacement. Small business owners in Texas face similar struggles when their growth plans get stuck because the system values speed over careful risk assessment. These delays hit vulnerable groups hardest—like elderly policyholders who may not be tech-savvy or low-income families who can't afford to fight a denial. To prevent harm, insurers should set up safeguards such as mandatory human reviews for high-stakes cases, regular bias checks on AI models, and easy-to-follow appeals processes. Without these protections, AI in insurance could end up prioritizing profits over fairness, transparency, and trust.

**The program started with a single underwriter in 2021.** Three years later, Hancock Underwriters’ “Hank” AI assistant is live on 62% of in-force policies and processes 42% of all endorsement requests without human touch. Hancock—an MGA writing primarily admitted E&S property in Texas and Florida—reported a combined ratio improvement from 98.2 in 2022 to 94.7 in 2023 after the rollout. [Hancock UW 2023 Annual Report, March 2024]

**Context: Who Hancock Underwriters is and why they moved first** Hancock is a two-decade-old MGA with roughly $1.1 B in premium under management and a 14-person underwriting team. Prior to 2021, every submission and endorsement crossed an underwriter’s desk. Average cycle time for a mid-market property endorsement was 3.2 business days. The team handled 2,100 endorsements in 2020, a volume that grew 29% YoY. Texas wind and hail exposure plus Florida sinkhole exclusions created a perfect storm: brokers demanded faster bind-and-quote cycles while reinsurers tightened T&C language. Hancock’s CFO challenged the underwriting team to cut endorsement cycle time in half without hiring another full-time underwriter.

**Challenge: Underwriters burned 41% of their time on routine servicing work** I spent a week shadowing Hancock’s underwriting floor in Q1 2021. Out of every 20-minute block, underwriters spent: - 11 minutes reviewing loss runs, inspection reports, and prior policy language - 6 minutes validating forms and state filings - 4 minutes updating the bordereaux and pushing data into four downstream systems The remaining 29% was actual risk assessment and pricing. Hancock’s loss ratio on serviced policies was drifting upward because the team could not spend enough time on red-flagged accounts.

**Solution: A “narrow copilot” that sits inside the underwriter’s core system** The architecture reflects a deliberate, modular design where a Python microservice interfaces with a fine-tuned LLM on AWS Bedrock, augmented by a vector store containing Hancock’s policy language, Texas DOI rules, and reinsurance treaties. The system’s shallow depth ensures rapid interpretability and control, but the *feedback loops* extend beyond the underwriter’s desk—the copilot’s outputs influence underwriting decisions, which in turn shape future policy language and regulatory interpretations. When the system responds by enforcing authority limits or flagging endorsements for review, it creates *emergent behavior* in underwriting workflows, potentially standardizing risk acceptance patterns or shifting manual review bottlenecks elsewhere in the chain. The integration of a deterministic rule engine acts as a stabilizing mechanism, preventing unintended second-order effects from cascading into reinsurance or claims adjudication.

Let me put that in context. The model is safeguarded by Hancock’s governance board, structured in lockstep with the Texas DOI’s August 2022 “model use in underwriting” guidance. For statistical rigor, Hancock tracks monthly performance on a holdout cohort of 1,200 endorsed policies sampled from 2020—because, statistically, a holdout set of n=1,200 gives us ~95% confidence intervals tight enough to detect drift before it becomes a problem. In Q3 2023, the endorsement-pricing drift measured 0.4% (p < 0.05 against the August 2022 baseline), comfortably above Hancock’s internal 0.3% drift threshold, so the model went into a controlled rollback until the vector store was refreshed with the latest reinsurance schedules. The numbers don’t lie—even a 0.4% drift on a portfolio of this size translates to a potential $3.4 MM annualized impact at 95% CI.

Hancock’s CFO shared a two-year ROI model, but let’s be honest—what if this model is dangerously optimistic? Here’s the uncomfortable truth: most people miss that these numbers hinge on 3,100 annual endorsements. **But what if the real volume is barely half that?** The conventional wisdom is wrong here—if Hancock can’t hit volume targets, that $412K NPV evaporates fast while the $89 underwriter cost and $0.34 cloud inference barely budge.

The model’s breakeven point rises to 18 months if endorsement volume declines 15% or if reinspection frequency increases more than 8%.

Here's what I tell my team about lessons learned from real-world experience: Narrow your focus instead of aiming too broad. Hancock tried to build a full-stack underwriting assistant in 2021, but after spending $220K and six months, they scrapped it. The key insight is to start with high-frequency, low-risk tasks—like endorsements—before moving on to more complex areas like quotes or new business. The 2023 SMA survey backs this up: 68% of MGAs that tried automating multiple products gave up within a year.

2. **Data quality gates the model more than the model itself** In Q1 2022, the model approved an endorsement that omitted a mandatory sinkhole exclusion. The error was traced to an outdated Texas sinkhole map in the vector store. Hancock now refreshes all state regulatory documents monthly and runs a deterministic pre-check before any LLM call. This single control reduced approval errors from 2.1% to 0.3%.

3. **Broker adoption is the real bottleneck** Hancock initially assumed brokers would use the embedded UI inside Guidewire. Instead, 63% of brokers continued emailing PDFs. Hancock built a lightweight broker portal in Q3 2022, but adoption only reached 47% after they added e-signature and real-time status push to Slack/Teams. The takeaway: insurtech tools must integrate into the broker’s existing workflow, not force a new one.

**Model drift is not a future risk—it’s a present cost center** Hancock’s shift from quarterly to monthly model refreshes after the reinsurer’s 2023 update to Florida’s wind deductible schedule reveals how *local changes propagate systemically*. The reinsurer’s policy tweak altered underwriting inputs, triggering a *feedback loop* where Hancock’s models degraded until recalibrated, directly inflating cloud costs and operational overhead. The $12K refresh price tag and 8% of cloud spend allocated to drift remediation highlight a hidden but critical *cost of system interconnectivity*—where a single regulatory or contractual update can cascade into financial and computational penalties. Vendors promising “set-and-forget” solutions ignore these *second-order effects*; without transparent refresh schedules, insurers risk unmanaged drift that propagates through underwriting, pricing, and ultimately, claims outcomes. The system responds by absorbing inefficiency until intervention becomes unavoidable.

**What’s next: Hancock’s 2024 expansion roadmap** Hancock is piloting a “Hank Lite” version for their small commercial book (GL and BOP). They expect to process 25% of endorsements automatically by year-end, but they’re not chasing 100% automation, and their CFO told me bluntly: “Above 55% no-touch, the marginal ROI flips negative because we still need human eyes for outliers.” They’re also evaluating parametric triggers for wind hail claims, but the CTO is skeptical: “Parametric sounds elegant until the broker miscodes the trigger location and we pay a claim we shouldn’t.”

The hard question every MGA should ask Hancock’s team next: at what endorsement volume does the model’s fixed refresh cost overwhelm the labor savings? Hancock’s breakeven flips at 2,500 annual endorsements; smaller MGAs may never cross that line.
