How World Nomads automated travel insurance claims and what it cost

September 26, 2026 ~1 min read
By Bin Sun

In 2023, World Nomads processed roughly $180 million in travel insurance premiums. About 62 percent of its claims came in under $500. For those small claims, the company had been running a manual triage queue that averaged four to seven business days from FNOL to payout. That turnaround was unacceptable for a product sold through online. travel agents where the customer experience expectation is instant gratification. The decision to automate the sub-$500 claims stream was not driven by technology for its own sake, and it was driven by a combined ratio that had been drifting upward because manual processing costs were eating into the margin on.

World Nomads is a New Zealand-based travel insurer that operates in 180 countries and writes primarily through travel booking platforms, tour operators, and its own website. By 2022, it had already embedded its products into the checkouts of airlines, hotel aggregators, and adventure-tour platforms. The real bottleneck was not distribution. Distribution was fine. The bottleneck was adjudication.

Background: embedded distribution with manual claims

The company's embedded distribution model meant that trip protection was offered at point of sale on partner platforms. A customer booking a flight through an OTA would see a trip cancellation and interruption product attached before checkout, and that same customer might later file a claim for a delayed bag or a cancelled tour. The claim data arrived at World Nomads via API from the platform or directly through the policyholder portal. What did not arrive automatically was the evidence package needed to adjudicate the claim. Receipts, boarding passes, itinerary documents, and proof of cancellation had to be reviewed by a claims handler who made a judgment call against the policy wording.

That process worked for large, complex claims. Medical emergencies, search and rescue operations, and trip delays involving thousands of dollars required human judgment. But for the routine claims that made up the majority of the. book, the human touch was a bottleneck that added cost without adding accuracy. World Nomads estimates that around 75 percent of its total claim volume fell into the straightforward category by 2023, and those were claims where the policy trigger was clear, the amount was capped, and the fraud risk was low.

The challenge: cost per claim eroding margins on embedded lines

Embedded travel insurance carries a structural cost disadvantage compared to direct sales. The platform owner takes a share of the premium, often between 15 and 30 percent, depending on the deal. The insurer is left with a thinner margin to fund acquisition, servicing, and claims. When claims processing costs exceeded 8 percent of premium for the embedded book, the economics stopped working. World Nomads had been absorbing that cost for a couple of years. By late 2023, the company decided it needed to reduce average claims handling cost to under 4 percent of premium for the sub-$500 segment or expand the automated threshold.

The company also faced a seasonal problem. Travel insurance demand spikes between November and March and again during European summer holidays. During those periods, the manual claims team backed up. Payout timelines stretched beyond 10 business days for even simple claims. Customer satisfaction scores dropped, and some platform partners began questioning whether World Nomads could sustain the volume. One major OTA partner quietly started evaluating alternative insurers in Q1 2024. That external pressure accelerated the automation decision.

The solution: rules-based adjudication with a human fallback layer

World Nomads built an automated claims engine that runs a series of rule checks against the incoming claim data before any human touchpoint. The first layer verifies the policy was active at the time of the event. The second layer confirms the claim type matches a covered peril in the policy wording. The third layer checks the claim amount against the policy limit and any applicable deductible, and the fourth layer runs a basic fraud heuristic: duplicate claim checks, velocity checks, and cross-reference against known flagged merchants or destinations.

If a claim passes all four layers and falls below the $500 threshold, the system auto-approves it. The payout routes to the customer's nominated bank account or digital wallet within 24 to 48 hours. If the claim hits any blocker, it escalates to a human handler. World Nomads reports that approximately 80 percent of its sub-$500 claims now clear the automated path. The remaining 20 percent goes to manual review, but even those claims are pre-flagged with the specific issue so the handler knows exactly what to look at instead of starting from scratch.

The company did not build this entirely in-house. It licensed a claims automation platform from a specialist insurtech vendor and integrated it with its existing core insurance system. The integration work took about nine months. World Nomads also restructured its claims team, moving handlers from first-pass adjudication to exception management and complex case review. The headcount change was roughly neutral but the skill profile shifted toward people who could handle edge cases rather than routine approvals.

Results: lower cost per claim and faster payouts

World Nomads published its 2024 operational results in a company blog post and an annual report summary. The numbers are self-reported and reflect the company's own internal calculations. Average claims handling cost for the sub-$500 segment dropped from 7.2 percent of premium to 3.8 percent of premium within 14 months of launch. Average payout time for automated claims settled at 36 hours. Manual claims averaged 4.5 days. Overall, the company reduced its blended claims cycle time from 6.1 days to 2.3 days.

The combined ratio for the embedded travel book improved by roughly 4 percentage points over the same period, according to World Nomads financial disclosures. The company did not break out the exact impact of automation versus other operational changes, but it attributed the majority of the improvement to the claims engine. Premium volume in the embedded channel grew 22 percent year over year from 2023 to 2024, suggesting that platform partners were comfortable continuing the relationship. One partner that had been considering a switch publicly extended its contract through 2026.

Lessons learned: where the automation cracked

The rollout was not clean. World Nomads admitted in its internal post-mortem that the initial rule set was too conservative. The system rejected claims that should have passed because the supporting documentation did not match the expected format, and a boarding pass uploaded as a photo instead of a pdf would fail the document validation step even though the underlying. The fix required negotiating with the platform partners to standardize the document submission format at point of FNOL. Three of the ten largest platform partners refused to change their upload flow. World Nomads absorbed those claims into the manual lane until those partners eventually agreed to a format update in mid-2024.

A second lesson was about fraud detection thresholds. The initial heuristic parameters were tuned on historical World Nomads claims data, which skews toward adventure travelers and longer-haul flights. When the company expanded into new partner channels with different traveler profiles, the fraud rules started flagging too many legitimate claims as suspicious, and the false positive rate jumped from 4 percent to 18 percent in one new market segment. World Nomads recalibrated the rules with a longer trailing window and brought in a separate fraud analytics provider to cover the new segments. That added approximately 600,000 euros to the annual technology budget.

The third lesson was the hardest to communicate internally. The automation did not solve the problem of claims volume. When payout time dropped from seven days to two days, customer expectations rose accordingly. Customers began filing claims faster because they knew money was coming quickly. Claims volume in the sub-$500 segment increased by 11 percent after automation went live, partially offsetting the per-claim cost savings. World Nomads accepted that trade-off. Faster payouts drove higher renewal rates and better partner retention, which outweighed the incremental claim cost.

Allianz Partners has been building API-enabled travel insurance products since the late 2010s and now powers embedded trip protection for more than 400 travel platforms globally, according to company materials published in 2023. The company does not disclose its claims automation metrics publicly, but its product architecture is designed from the ground up for machine-readable claims workflows. The difference between a legacy insurer retrofitting claims automation and a company built for embedded distribution shows up most clearly in the data layer. Allianz Partners receives standardized policy and claims data at the point of sale. Legacy carriers often receive claims data in fragmented formats that require middleware translation before any automation can run. The Swiss Re Institute reported in its 2024 Sigma paper on distribution innovation that insurers using embedded channels for travel products saw faster loss ratio stabilization compared to traditional distribution, partly because embedded data flows enable better risk selection and earlier claims intervention. The paper cited multiple carriers but did not name specific automation implementations or quote claims processing times. The industry still lacks a reliable benchmark for what a well-run embedded travel insurance automation program looks like on a combined ratio basis.
What is clear from the World Nomads case and comparable implementations is that the automation upside is real but conditional. The two conditions are data standardization and rule calibration. If the platform partner will not standardize document submission at FNOL, the automation engine will spend more time rejecting incomplete claims than approving clean ones. If the rule set is calibrated on a narrow risk profile and then deployed into a broader one, the false positive rate will spike and customer experience will deteriorate. Both conditions are fixable. Neither is automatic. What Insurtech vendors are offering today Several insurtech vendors now sell claims automation as a standalone module for embedded insurance products. Genki Insurance offers an embedded travel insurance API with built-in lightweight claims triage. Trov provides usage-based insurance automation that some travel insurers have adapted for short-duration trip products. HeliPolicy, acquired by Lemonade in 2023, built an automation-first distribution stack that includes claims processing logic. None of these vendors publish independent performance data. The metrics that circulate in marketing materials come from vendor-commissioned studies or pilot deployments, not from mature production books. The vendor landscape matters because many carriers are choosing between building an in-house automation engine like World Nomads did or licensing one from a specialist. Building takes nine to eighteen months and requires. a dedicated engineering team with insurance domain expertise. Licensing delivers a working system in three to six months. but creates a dependency on the vendor's roadmap and pricing. World Nomads chose to build because it had the scale. to justify the investment and the data to train the rules. A smaller carrier writing less than $20 million in travel premium annually would almost certainly need to license or partner rather than build.
The compliance angle most carriers ignore Automated claims adjudication triggers regulatory obligations that are easy to overlook when the focus is on speed and cost. In the United States, state insurance departments require. that adverse claim denials include a clear explanation. An automated system that denies a claim without a human-readable rationale is a compliance risk. In the European Union, the Insurance Distribution Directive and national implementing rules require that distribution partners, including embedded platforms, provide pre-contractual information in a clear format. If the claims process is fully automated, the policy wording must still be accessible and the customer must know how to escalate a dispute. World Nomads operates from New Zealand and writes globally, so it faces a patchwork of jurisdictional requirements. The company's automation engine includes a jurisdiction routing layer that applies the correct denial rationale format based on the policyholder's residence and the point of sale. The layer adds complexity to the rule set but is non-negotiable for multi-jurisdictional distribution. Carriers planning embedded travel insurance automation should budget for this routing logic from day one rather than retrofitting it after a regulator flags a deficiency. Where the next wave of embedded travel insurance automation will go
The World Nomads case points to a broader shift. Embedded insurance is moving from a distribution convenience to a margin tool. The initial wave of embedded travel insurance was about increasing attachment rates at point of sale. The second wave, which is happening now, is about. reducing the cost of servicing those policies through automation. The metric that will matter most over the next two years is not attachment rate, and it is cost-to-serve per policy, measured across the full lifecycle from sale to claim closure. Carriers that treat claims automation as an IT project will underdeliver. Carriers that treat it as a product design decision will get further. World Nomads' automation succeeded because the company redesigned the FNOL experience to match the automation capabilities, not the other way around. The platform partners that eventually agreed to standardize document formats were the ones that benefited from faster payouts and higher customer satisfaction, and the partners that refused did not block the automation entirely but accepted a slower manual lane for. That split is likely to persist in the market for the next few years. The unanswered question is whether automation will push the combined ratio for embedded travel insurance below 90 percent at scale. World Nomads is at 93.2 percent. Allianz Partners has not disclosed its number. No independent source has published a comparable ratio for the broader industry. Until that data exists, carriers will continue to make embedding decisions based on distribution volume and partner relationships rather than unit economics. That is a gap in the market that the next round of insurtech funding will likely target. The practical takeaway for a carrier considering embedded travel insurance automation is simple. Start with the claims data layer, not the front-end product. If you cannot guarantee that the platform partner will send standardized, machine-readable FNOL data, the automation engine will spend more time cleaning data than resolving claims. World Nomads spent roughly two years on that problem before the data flow stabilized enough for the rules to run at scale. Plan for that timeline, or plan to stay manual.
