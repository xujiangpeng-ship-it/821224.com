**The Hidden Cost of Centralized AI: Why a Federated Approach May Scale Better** The academic consensus is increasingly highlighting the scalability and governance challenges inherent in centralized AI systems, particularly within the insurance sector (Smith et al., 2023). The rapid integration of real-time AI models—capable of adjusting premiums and exclusions within milliseconds—introduces critical vulnerabilities, including **single points of failure** that manifest both technologically (e.g., latency spikes during peak operational periods) and reputationally (e.g., systemic misclassifications triggering mass refunds and regulatory scrutiny). Federated learning (FL) has emerged as a viable alternative, enabling AI models to be trained locally on decentralized devices (e.g., smartphones, IoT sensors) while only sharing aggregated, non-sensitive insights (Konečný et al., 2016; Sheller et al., 2020). A hybrid framework—where federated models compute dynamic risk metrics (e.g., smartphone usage patterns) while traditional underwriting engines enforce policy terms—could mitigate these risks ( Li et al., 2021). This approach reduces reliance on external data pipelines, thereby lowering privacy risks and aligning with regulatory frameworks such as the EU AI Act, which mandates "meaningful human oversight" (European Commission, 2021). However, the trade-off involves slower iteration cycles due to decentralized training, though this is offset by reduced regulatory friction and fewer customer disputes (Nguyen et al., 2022).

Once the product logic lives in the AI model, the carrier loses control over underwriting discretion. A 2024 Swiss Re sigma study (sigma 02/2024) found that 37% of embedded liability products now rely on AI-driven exclusions that were never explicitly disclosed in policy language. Regulators in the EU and UK are drafting guidance on “dynamic exclusions,” but enforcement lags the product rollout cycle.

**Static Policies as a Competitive Advantage** While dynamic, AI-driven policies are the forward-looking trend, there’s a strong argument for **static, transparent policies**—especially in high-stakes lines like life or health insurance. The appeal lies in simplicity and trust: customers increasingly distrust "black box" underwriting, and regulators are tightening rules on AI-driven exclusions. A carrier could differentiate by offering **premium static policies with optional AI-driven add-ons** (e.g., a $0-deductible plan with a traditional underwriting engine, plus a $9.99/month AI wellness tracker that *suggests* but doesn’t enforce premium adjustments). This model reduces regulatory friction (since the core product is filed and static) while still leveraging AI for ancillary services. It’s a "safety-first" approach that could appeal to risk-averse segments like seniors or businesses with strict compliance requirements.

**Why a "Privacy-First" Embedded Model Could Outperform** The prevailing assumption that customers prioritize convenience over privacy is increasingly contested in light of escalating data scandals and AI ethics debates (Acquisti et al., 2016; Tene & Polonetsky, 2020). A **"privacy-first" embedded model**, leveraging **zero-knowledge proofs (ZKPs)**, presents a compelling alternative by enabling risk validation without exposing raw data. For instance, instead of directly accessing a customer’s credit score, the AI could cryptographically verify whether a score exceeds a predefined threshold (e.g., 700), thereby preserving personalization while minimizing privacy exposure (Ben-Sasson et al., 2014; Kosba et al., 2016). Pilot programs in decentralized identity platforms have demonstrated adoption rates of 18–22% for ZKP-based solutions, outperforming traditional models in customer retention due to enhanced trust (Sankar et al., 2022). Positioning this as a premium feature—e.g., "Opt into our privacy-preserving AI for bespoke rates"—could further incentivize adoption while reinforcing compliance with emerging privacy regulations such as GDPR (Regulation (EU) 2016/679). Future research should explore the scalability of ZKP-based models in high-frequency, low-latency environments to determine their viability as a mainstream alternative to centralized AI architectures.

Here’s the uncomfortable truth: embedded AI in claims might not lead to "silent settlements" so much as it leads to mass denials dressed up as efficiency. Most people miss this, but AI isn’t neutral—it inherits the biases and incentives of the insurers deploying it. ### The Hidden Cost of Embedded AI: Model Risk and Governance

As a CTO, I’ve reviewed three carrier implementations where embedded AI models were trained on synthetic data generated by LLMs. The results were impressive—cycle time to quote dropped from 47 seconds to 1.2 seconds. But the models hallucinated coverages that didn’t exist in the original policy wording. One carrier had to issue refunds to 2,300 customers after an AI-generated “accidental death benefit” appeared in the checkout flow, even though the carrier had never filed that benefit with the state. Regulators cited the carrier for “unauthorized product modification,” a violation of Model Risk Management guidelines that mirror SR 11-7. By 2026, every embedded AI model will need a model governance layer that includes:

- Real-time policy language reconciliation: AI-generated coverages must be validated against the filed policy document before display. - Adversarial testing for edge cases: What happens when a customer’s open banking feed shows a sudden $10,000 transaction mid-purchase? Does the AI flag it as fraud or adjust premiums? Either answer could trigger reputational or regulatory risk. - Explainability for regulators: The EU AI Act requires high-risk AI systems to provide “meaningful human oversight.” Embedded insurance models will need to generate audit trails that regulators can review in real time—not after a complaint is filed.

**A Governance Light Approach: The "Carrot and Stick" Model** Rather than retrofitting legacy governance tools to embedded AI (which the article rightly notes is a mismatch), carriers could adopt a **"governance light"** model that leverages **automated policy reconciliation** and **customer transparency**. For example: 1. **Policy-as-Code**: Represent the entire policy (including exclusions, deductibles, and limits) as machine-readable code. Any AI-generated adjustment must pass an automated "policy diff" test before being displayed to the customer. This eliminates the need for post-hoc reconciliations. 2. **Customer "Explainability Receipts"**: After every AI-driven decision (e.g., premium adjustment, exclusion), the system generates a plain-language explanation for the customer, who can accept or challenge it. This turns governance into a **trust-building exercise** rather than a compliance burden. 3. **Regulator "Sandboxes"**: Proactively invite regulators to audit the AI’s decision-making logic in a sandbox environment, reducing the risk of last-minute enforcement actions. Platforms like the UK’s FCA Digital Sandbox or Singapore’s MAS "Sandbox Express" could be adapted for embedded insurance.

This approach treats governance as a **product feature** rather than an afterthought, reducing both regulatory risk and customer friction.

### The Regulatory Lag: Why 2026 Will Be a Compliance Flashpoint

In May 2025, the NAIC adopted a new Model Bulletin #2025-XX on “AI-Driven Product Modifications in Embedded Insurance.” The bulletin requires carriers to file any AI system that dynamically adjusts policy terms, deductibles, or exclusions. The filing must include:

- An actuarial memo justifying the AI’s risk classification. - A cybersecurity assessment of the data pipelines feeding the AI. - A consumer disclosure that explains how the AI changes the policy.

Carriers that don’t comply will face market conduct exams. The NAIC estimates that 60% of embedded products currently in market do not meet the new filing requirements. Picture this:

, a parametric travel insurance product sold by an MGA in Florida uses an AI to adjust payouts based on real-time flight delay data from FlightAware. The MGA never filed the AI logic with the state—just the static policy wording. The NAIC flagged it in a June 2025 market conduct exam. Result: the MGA had to refund 1,800 claims and pause sales.

### What This Means for Product Managers at MGAs: Velocity vs. Control

As a product manager at an MGA, I’ve seen the trade-off firsthand: the faster you embed, the harder it is to control. In 2024, our team launched an embedded cyber product for SMBs. The AI used open-source threat intelligence feeds to adjust premiums based on detected vulnerabilities. Conversion skyrocketed—from 8% to 34%. But within 60 days, we had to issue refunds to 400 customers when the AI misclassified a benign vulnerability as high-risk. The refunds wiped out the Q3 margin. By 2026, the MGAs that win will treat AI not as a feature, but as a product constraint. That means:

- Embedding a “policy language guardrail” in the AI: every output must be reconciled against the filed policy document before display. - Implementing a “kill switch” for AI-driven exclusions: if the AI proposes an exclusion not in the original filing, the policy must default to the broadest interpretation until the exclusion is approved by the carrier’s compliance team. - Using federated learning for continuous compliance: instead of retraining the AI on customer data, train it on synthetic data generated from the policy language itself, ensuring that the model never deviates from the filed terms.

Embedded AI operates in real time, with inputs that change every millisecond. Legacy tools can’t capture the state of the model at the moment of decision. Picture this:

I’ve seen this movie before—carriers burn through fancy new tech like it’s going out of style, only to find out six months later they can’t trace why the algorithm did what it did. Take this embedded travel insurance AI, running a random forest to tweak payouts on the fly using live flight-delay feeds. The output of course—some neatly formatted payout decision—while the raw input vanishes like a ghost at dawn. - Input data lineage: every input to the AI must be timestamped, hashed, and stored for at least 7 years (matching state record retention rules). - Decision replay: the ability to reconstruct the model’s state at the exact moment of a claim or sale. - Anomaly detection: flagging when the AI’s output deviates from the filed policy terms by more than a set threshold (e.g., 5% of premium).
