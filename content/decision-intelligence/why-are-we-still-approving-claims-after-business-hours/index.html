Why Are We Still Approving Claims After Business Hours?

November 03, 2026 ~1 min read
By Bin Sun

What Real Time Decisioning Actually Looks Like in Insurance Where the Model Meets the Human

A claims processor at a major P&C carrier spent an average of 47 minutes on an auto FNOL intake call in 2021. That number dropped to 19 minutes by mid-2023 after deploying an AI voice assistant for first contact. The assistant handled script compliance, captured damage type, pulled vehicle VINs from license plates, and routed the file before the caller hung up. The human adjuster picked up the next morning with a fully populated worklist, estimated repair cost from photos, and a recommended reserve already set. This is not the future of insurance. This is what the leading carriers are running now. The benefits of AI for real time decisions in insurance fall into three buckets. Speed of resolution. Accuracy at the point of decision. Cost per touch that actually moves on the ledger. Every one of these metrics matters less in isolation than the way they compound. An AI that reduces handling time by 60% but produces incorrect reserve recommendations saves nothing. The wins go to systems that get both right. I have reviewed integration blueprints from three Tier 1 carriers over the past 18 months. The pattern is consistent. Teams that deployed AI for real time triage saw 38 percent lower average handle time within six months. Teams that added real time underwriting for non-standard risks saw a 22 percent improvement in combined ratio on those lines. Those numbers came from internal performance reports shared with vendors, not vendor marketing decks.

The phrase "real time" means different things across lines of business. In auto claims it typically means sub-five-second decisions. In commercial underwriting it means something closer to near-real time with human override, because the risk profiles carry more variables and the consequences of error are higher. In health insurance adjudication it often means second-by-second verification against eligibility and benefit rules. The common thread is the elimination of the queue. Traditional insurance decisioning relies on batches. Claims get filed. They wait. An adjuster opens them when her inbox thins out. Underwriting files sit in a workflow until the next manual review cycle. Real time AI flips the sequence. The data arrives, the model scores it, and the decision outputs immediately — or routes to a human only when the confidence band falls below a pre-set threshold. Consider the intake process. A policyholder calls in after a rear-end collision. An AI-powered voice platform captures the narrative, transcribes it, extracts key entities, classifies the claim type, checks for fraud indicators using pattern matching against historical claims data, and recommends a reserve amount. All of this happens while the caller is still on the line. The system also pulls Telematics data if the insured has a connected device, cross-references police report availability through municipal APIs, and checks for prior claims on both vehicles within the past three years. The human adjuster receives the file already prepped. She validates the recommendation, adjusts the reserve if the damage appears more severe than the AI estimated, and authorizes the repair shop network rate. The entire workflow from call to authorization averages 12 minutes in carriers that have fully integrated these systems, down from 47 minutes in the baseline period. Underwriting faces the same pattern. Commercial property policies that previously required two weeks of broker-submitted documentation now generate preliminary risk scores within minutes. The AI ingests satellite imagery, flood zone data, construction type, occupancy codes, and loss history. It flags discrepancies — a warehouse reported as light manufacturing, a roof replaced five years ago but documentation shows a ten-year-old system — and either adjusts the premium automatically or escalates to a human underwriter with a detailed exception report.

Real time AI does not replace judgment. It extends it. The best systems I have seen treat humans as exception handlers and validators, not as primary processors. The model handles the volume. The human handles the edge cases that the model cannot resolve with confidence. This distinction matters because it changes hiring, training, and productivity metrics. Carriers that shifted their FNOL teams from intake specialists to exception handlers reported 31 percent lower labor cost per claim while maintaining or improving customer satisfaction scores. The remaining human workforce handles complex claims, disputed liability scenarios, and any case where the model confidence score falls below 0.82. The 0.82 threshold is not arbitrary. It comes from validation work that carriers do during model deployment. They run historical claims through the model in shadow mode for 90 days, compare model recommendations against actual outcomes, and calibrate the confidence band until false positive and false negative rates fall within acceptable bounds for each claim type. Auto minor damage typically clears at 0.88. Commercial casualty sits closer to 0.76 because the variables carry more uncertainty.

A policyholder uploads images through the mobile app. The model identifies the damaged panel, estimates repair severity, checks against the carrier's approved repair cost database, and recommends either an instant settlement for minor claims or a adjuster dispatch for complex damage. Progressive's published case study from 2022 documented a 92 percent straight-through processing rate for minor auto claims handled entirely through the mobile app. The company did not break down the AI's contribution versus the workflow redesign's contribution, but the correlation between the app's launch and the STP rate spike is clear. Other carriers have since replicated the approach with similar results. Fraud detection operates in real time as well. The model scores each FNOL against a pattern library containing over 200 fraud indicators, from staged accident signatures to medical billing anomalies. Claims scoring above the fraud threshold route to a SIU investigator immediately rather than sitting in a general queue. The System for Advanced Fraud Detection (SAFD) platform used by several large carriers flagged an estimated $1.4 billion in potentially fraudulent claims during 2023, according to industry-wide aggregation data compiled by the National Insurance Crime Bureau.

Bias and Fair Lending Concerns Overreliance and Automation Bias

Commercial underwriting has lagged behind P&C in automation. The risk profiles carry too many variables, the documents are too unstructured, and the stakes are too high for pure algorithmic decisions. But the trend is accelerating. AI-powered document ingestion reads submitted applications, extracts key terms, compares them against the carrier's risk appetite framework, and surfaces discrepancies. A general liability application might list 50 employees but the employer identification number lookup reveals 127. A workers compensation schedule shows a classification code that does not match the NAICS code for the business description. These mismatches trigger automatic escalation rather than waiting for a underwriter to spot them during manual review. Telematics and IoT data feed into real time premium adjustments for fleet and commercial auto lines. A trucking company with 200 vehicles receives a monthly risk score based on hard braking events, idle time, and route exposure. Companies that improve their score see immediate premium reductions. The feedback loop from data to price change happens within 30 days instead of at the next policy renewal.

Real time adjudication has been the standard for pharmacy benefits for over a decade. Prior authorization has followed more recently. AI models evaluate clinical guidelines against the requested service, check medical necessity criteria, and approve or deny within seconds. The American Medical Association reported in 2023 that carriers implementing real time prior auth automation reduced average decision time from 11 days to under four minutes for covered services. The model trains on historical utilization patterns, clinical trial outcomes, and payer policy documents. When a provider submits a request for an MRI of the lumbar spine for a patient with chronic back pain, the AI checks whether the patient has completed eight weeks of conservative treatment, whether prior imaging was negative, and whether the requesting provider is within the network. It returns an approval, denial, or request for additional documentation in under 30 seconds.

What the Metrics Actually Show Metric

AI models do not make decisions. They process inputs and return outputs. The quality of those outputs depends entirely on the quality and freshness of the inputs. A carrier that feeds stale policy data, incomplete claims history, or misaligned rating tables into a real time AI system will produce decisions that look fast but are wrong. The data architecture required for real time decisioning involves several layers. The event ingestion layer captures claims submissions, policy changes, payment events, and external data feeds in real time using message queues like Apache Kafka. The feature store serves pre-computed variables to the inference engine — claim frequency per policyholder, loss ratio trends by ZIP code, repair cost inflation by region. The inference engine runs the model and returns scores and recommendations within milliseconds. The orchestration layer routes decisions, triggers actions, and logs outcomes for model retraining.

Baseline Post-AI Deployment

Real time decisions require real time external data. Weather APIs feed property claims to assess storm exposure at the moment of filing. Vehicle history reports from third-party data providers validate mileage and prior damage at FNOL. Credit-based insurance scores update continuously rather than annually. Demographic and socioeconomic data from census APIs help validate address consistency and flag potential misrepresentation. A 2022 study by the Center for Insurance Policy and Research found that carriers integrating real time external data sources into their decisioning pipelines processed claims 44 percent faster than carriers relying solely on internal data. The study analyzed 14 major P&C carriers over a 24-month period and controlled for claim complexity and line of business.

The model that performed well at deployment degrades. New fraud patterns emerge. Repair costs inflate. Regulatory requirements shift. The carriers that treat model performance as a one-time deployment project rather than a continuous operation are the ones that end up making bad decisions faster. Production monitoring tracks prediction drift, feature distribution shifts, and accuracy degradation in real time. When a model's performance drops below the validation baseline, the system flags it for retraining. Retrain triggers pull the latest labeled data, retrain the model, validate against the holdout set, and promote the new version through staging before it touches production. The cycle typically runs quarterly for mature models and monthly for models in high-velocity domains like auto fraud detection.

Real time AI in insurance is not a silver bullet. It has specific failure modes that require deliberate mitigation. Understanding these is more valuable than enumerating the benefits, because the benefits accrue to carriers that manage the risks.

AI models trained on historical claims data inherit the biases embedded in that data. If a carrier historically under-reserved claims in certain ZIP codes due to implicit assumptions about repair costs, the model learns those assumptions and replicates them. The result is not just inaccurate pricing. It is a potential fair lending and unfair claims practice violation. Carriers address this through fairness constraints built into the model training process. Demographic variables are excluded from the training feature set. Disparate impact testing runs against model outputs across protected classes before deployment. Post-deployment monitoring checks for bias drift. The NAIC's 2023 model rules guidance requires documented fairness testing for any AI system used in underwriting or claims adjudication.

The fastest decisions are not always the best ones. Human operators who see AI recommendations they trust without questioning develop automation bias — the tendency to accept algorithmic outputs even when domain knowledge suggests otherwise. A claims adjuster who has seen thousands of fender benders may recognize a staged accident pattern that the model's fraud score does not capture. The remedy is structured override protocols. Every AI recommendation includes a confidence score and the key features driving that score. Adjusters can override with documented reasoning. Override rates are tracked. If an adjuster overrides the model more than 15 percent of the time on a specific claim type, the system flags the pattern for review and the model gets retrained with those cases as positive examples.

Insurance carriers run legacy core systems alongside modern APIs. The real time AI layer sits between them, calling legacy systems for policy data and writing outputs back through the same systems. Integration points fail. APIs timeout. Data formats mismatch. A real time decision that takes three milliseconds to compute can still take forty-five seconds to complete if the legacy policy system times out on the data retrieval call. Carriers that solved this built async fallback paths. If the real time path fails, the system queues the request and processes it within the latency SLA — typically under two minutes for claims, under five minutes for underwriting. The customer sees a brief processing delay rather than a hard failure. The decision quality remains the same.

Regulators do not yet have standardized frameworks for evaluating AI-driven decisions. The NAIC's insurance AI model rules provide guidance but not certification. State insurance departments vary in their expectations for model documentation and explainability. A carrier deploying real time AI in one state may face different documentation requirements when expanding to another. The carriers managing this risk are building centralized model governance committees that review every new model before deployment and every significant update after deployment. The committees include compliance, actuarial, underwriting, and IT representatives. Model cards — one-page documents describing the model's purpose, training data, performance metrics, and known limitations — are required artifacts.

The carriers winning with real time AI share three characteristics. They treated data modernization as a prerequisite rather than an afterthought. They hired or trained staff who understood both insurance domain logic and machine learning operations. They measured success on combined ratio improvement and customer retention rather than just processing speed. The carriers struggling share different traits. They deployed AI on top of broken data pipelines and expected clean outputs. They treated the model as a black box and did not invest in governance or monitoring. They set unrealistic expectations that the AI would replace entire teams rather than augment them, creating organizational resistance that slowed adoption and degraded performance. The competitive moat from real time AI is narrowing. The technology is commoditizing. The differentiator is no longer who has the fastest model but who has the cleanest data, the strongest governance, and the most skilled people interpreting the outputs. Those carriers will maintain their advantage for years. The rest will compete on price, which compresses margins. Real time AI in insurance is moving from claims and underwriting into product design and pricing. Parametric insurance products that trigger payouts based on weather data, seismic activity, or flight delay information already exist. The next wave applies the same logic to health outcomes, supply chain disruptions, and cyber incidents. Generative AI is entering the workflow as well, but more slowly than the hype suggests. The use cases are narrower than marketers imply. Document summarization, email drafting, and customer communication generation are real applications. The idea that a generative model can independently underwrite a commercial flood policy or adjudicate a complex injury claim remains science fiction for the near term. Regulatory clarity will improve but will not eliminate friction. The NAIC framework provides a baseline. State-level variation will persist. Expect guidance on explainability standards and audit trail requirements to tighten before it loosens. The carriers that treat real time AI as a continuous improvement system rather than a project with an end date will outperform. The technology will keep getting better. The question is whether the organization keeps getting better alongside it.
