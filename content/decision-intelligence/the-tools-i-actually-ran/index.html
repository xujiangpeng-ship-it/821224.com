Decision Intelligence

The tools I actually ran

November 27, 2026 ~1 min read
By Bin Sun

The best AI tools for fraud decision in 2026 aren't in the model layer. I've spent the last 18 months replacing two legacy rules engines with ML-based decisioning at a mid-size P&C carrier, and the tools that actually moved our fraud loss ratio were boring. The ones with the flashier demos in the Gartner Market Guide for Fraud Detection and Prevention (2024) were the ones that stalled in UAT because nobody on the vendor side had ever touched a bordereaux file. So this review is built from production deployments, not slide decks. I'm covering five platforms I've run or stress-tested: NICE Actimize, FICO Decision Management, SAS Viya Fraud Framework, DataRobot, and Exigent Systems. I'm a data science lead, so I'm evaluating these on feature engineering workflow, model governance, data pipeline resilience, and whether the thing that worked in the sandbox still works when the FNOL volume triples after a cat event.

I've seen projects where just the data extraction from the claims system ate four months. The vendor will not tell you that. Setup: where the vendor deck lies
Every one of these vendors ships with a 20-page "rapid deployment" guide. None of them account for the actual mess of an insurance data environment. With NICE Actimize, the setup assumed our policy administration system and our claims system shared a unified claimant identifier. They didn't. Reconciling those two took my team six weeks of SQL joins and a spreadsheet nobody wanted to document. The vendor's professional services team was available for that, but at a day rate that made our CFO re-evaluate the entire project. FICO Decision Management was cleaner on the integration side because it bolts onto your existing decision engine rather than replacing it. But the learning curve for the decision logic builder is steeper than the marketing suggests. I spent two days just figuring out how to model a parametric trigger for commercial auto, and the documentation was written for a generalist, not a data scientist working in a P&C context. SAS Viya Fraud Framework is the heaviest lift. The network analytics module is genuinely good at catching organized fraud rings (linked claimants, shared adjuster assignments, geographic clustering), but it needs graph-structured data that most carriers don't maintain. You'll be building the graph layer yourself before you can run a single model. That's not a criticism of SAS. That's a criticism of how the insurance industry structures its data. But it does mean your "8 to 14 months" timeline is really "14 to 22 months" unless you've already invested in a graph database. DataRobot is the fastest to get running. I had a working model on 1,200 labeled fraud claims in under three weeks. The autoML pipeline is the most polished I've used. But "fastest to get running" and "fit for a regulated decisioning environment" are different things, and I'll get to that. Exigent Systems was the only tool that came with pre-built claims fraud models tuned for US personal lines. The setup was almost trivial: point it at your claims API, map a few fields, and it started scoring. Almost trivial. Then you discover the scoring threshold defaults are conservative enough that your SIU team will ignore the top 300 alerts because the false positive rate in week one was embarrassing.
What they do well NICE Actimize's hybrid rules-plus-ML approach is the most defensible in a regulatory audit. When the OIG or state DOI asks "why did you decline this claim?", you can point to both the rule that fired and the model probability. The explainability layer is the best in the group, and for a carrier writing in multiple states, that's not a luxury. It's the difference between a 3-week audit and a 9-month one. FICO's strength is velocity. If you're launching a new product line and need a fraud decisioning layer in six weeks, FICO's pre-built policy decision templates get you 70% of the way there. The 400-series decision models for commercial auto and workers' comp are solid starting points. You won't beat a custom model eventually, but you'll be in production while your custom model is still in backtesting. SAS Viya's anomaly detection on the network layer is the only tool in this group that found a 14-claim organized ring in our commercial property book that our SIU team had been manually chasing for eight months. The graph traversal is fast enough to run on a near-real-time schedule against 2M+ edges, which matters when you're monitoring inbound FNOL for cat events (if you know, you know). DataRobot won on feature engineering. The auto-generated feature set from unstructured fields (adjuster narrative, police report text) produced a 4.2-point lift in fraud detection recall over our rules baseline. That lift translated to roughly $1.8M in identified recoveries in the first quarter of production, per our finance team's reconciliation. It's the strongest ROI number I can put on a single tool decision.
Exigent's SIU referral workflow is the tightest. The handoff from "model flagged this claim" to "SIU analyst gets a case file with the top-5 risk factors explained in plain English" is seamless. No copy-paste, no ticket system. The analysts actually open the referrals. Where they fall short None of these tools handle model drift gracefully. I retrained a NICE Actimize model in month five, and the performance curve on our validation set looked fine. Then a new cat event hit in month seven, shifted the base-rate fraud prevalence by 12 points, and the model's precision dropped enough that the false positive flood made our adjusters stop trusting the scores. There's no auto-retrain trigger built in. You have to schedule it yourself, and the monitoring dashboards are basic. SAS Viya is slightly better here, but still manual. If your fraud environment is non-stationary (and it is, every cat season), you're building your own MLOps layer on top. FICO's per-decision pricing gets expensive at volume. Our carrier processes roughly 400K first-party claims annually. At the mid-tier volume bracket, the annual cost was in the high six figures before we added the two custom decision models we needed for our specialty lines. I ran the unit economics and the cost per decision exceeded the savings on 30% of flagged claims. The tool pays for itself on the high-value commercial book. It does not pay for itself on personal auto. DataRobot's autoML is a double-edged sword. The feature engineering is fast, but when a regulator asks you to produce the exact model specification, the version control in the platform is adequate but not audit-grade. I've had to export model cards manually for two state filings. You want that baked in. It isn't.

Exigent's claims-only focus means it's useless for pre-sale fraud (application fraud, UW fraud). If your fraud problem is 80% post-sale claims, it's the right tool. If it's 50/50, you need a second platform, and the integration between them is on you.

Pricing and the cost line nobody budgets for

The license or subscription is line one. The real cost is the data engineering work around it. I've watched teams budget $200K for a fraud platform and then spend $300K on the data pipeline, the feature store, the model registry, and the monitoring dashboards that make the platform actually usable. Gartner's 2024 Market Guide for Fraud Detection and Prevention calls this out in its "total cost of ownership" section, but the guidance is generic. What I'd tell a CFO: budget 1.5x the sticker price for the first year, because the integration and governance work is non-optional and it's where the project actually dies.

For a mid-market P&C carrier with 500K claims annually, the realistic all-in cost for a production-grade fraud decisioning stack (one of the tools above plus the supporting infrastructure) sits between $400K and $700K per year. That's before you add the SIU analyst time to act on the alerts. If your fraud loss ratio is above 45% of total claims loss, the math works. If it's 30%, you're buying a compliance expense and you should say so to the board, not frame it as a technology investment.

The question I haven't solved

None of these tools, as of Q1 2026, handle adversarial adaptation. Organized fraud rings are learning faster than our retrain cycle. We retrain quarterly. The networks I'm seeing in the SAS graph outputs are restructuring their patterns every 6 to 8 weeks. The gap between our model refresh cadence and the adversary's adaptation speed is the open problem I can't close with any tool on this list. I'm currently prototyping a continuous-monitoring pipeline that re-triggers retraining when the network topology shifts, but it's 80% built and the last 20% is a governance question: who signs off on a model that retrains itself mid-quarter? The answer is probably my compliance team, which means we're back to a human-in-the-loop bottleneck that defeats the purpose of automation.

If you're evaluating these tools this year, I'd start by pulling 18 months of your claims data, building the graph layer in-house, and running it through SAS Viya's anomaly detection before you commit to a platform. The graph is the moat. The model is replaceable. The data structure is not, and it's the piece every vendor assumes you already have.
