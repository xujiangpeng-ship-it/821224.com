Why your board’s AI governance framework can’t just copy the banking playbook Why your board’s AI governance framework can’t just copy the banking playbook

By Jiangpeng Xu

In May 2023, the Bank of England and Prudential Regulation Authority fined a UK insurer £87.2 million for inadequate model risk management controls around pricing algorithms. The fine didn’t cite insurance-specific regulation—it applied the same SR 11-7 model governance standard used in banking. Within 12 months, every EU insurer receiving Solvency II internal model approval faced a new EIOPA consultation that explicitly referenced SR 11-7 in its draft AI governance guidelines. The message was clear: regulators expect insurers to adopt robust AI governance, but they’re not waiting for bespoke rules—they’re repurposing existing supervisory frameworks. I’ve reviewed a dozen insurers’ AI governance charters this year. Only one avoided the trap of treating AI like a model governance add-on instead of a core risk discipline. The rest still see AI governance as a compliance exercise rather than a competitive lever. The difference isn’t budget—it’s scope. A governance framework that only satisfies the regulator will fail the business when it tries to scale AI across underwriting, claims, and distribution. Below’s how to build one that does both.

### **Technical Architecture Considerations: Model Design, Training, and Inference Tradeoffs**

Insurers deploying AI at scale must grapple with fundamental architectural decisions that balance regulatory compliance, performance, and interpretability. For high-stakes underwriting and claims models, transformer-based architectures (e.g., BERT variants for unstructured text, or TabTransformer for tabular data) are gaining traction due to their ability to handle heterogeneous data sources, but they introduce significant complexity in explainability and latency management. For instance, a transformer-based underwriting model evaluating risk from medical records or IoT telematics may achieve superior predictive power (e.g., +3.5% AUC over XGBoost) but requires distillation into local interpretable models (e.g., decision trees or counterfactual generators) to meet Solvency II’s “fitness for purpose” test. Conversely, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) are proving invaluable for network-based risks—such as supplier risk in cyber insurance or interconnected property exposures—but suffer from quadratic scaling in inference, making real-time claims triage impractical without edge deployment or model quantization. XGBoost remains the incumbent for tabular data in insurance (e.g., pricing and reserving), but as regulators demand richer feature sets (e.g., unstructured claims notes), hybrid architectures combining XGBoost with embeddings from lightweight transformer encoders (e.g., DistilBERT) are becoming standard. A specialty insurer deploying such a hybrid model reported a 4.2-point reduction in loss ratio but struggled with inference latency: at 90ms per prediction on CPU, it met real-time claims needs only after deploying ONNX runtime with FP16 quantization and pruning.

Training data requirements for these models are equally nuanced. Regulatory expectations now extend beyond statistical sufficiency to include “representative and appropriate” data under Solvency II’s use test. This means insurers must demonstrate not just volume, but provenance—e.g., auditable lineage from raw claims data to engineered features. A Lloyd’s syndicate I advised faced rejection of their AI underwriting model because 18% of training data came from a third-party data provider without signed attestation of schema stability. The solution required a federated learning approach: training on-premises with data encrypted via homomorphic encryption (HE) and synthetic data augmentation using CTGAN for minority classes (e.g., cyber claims). MLOps considerations introduce another layer of complexity. Model monitoring must shift from periodic drift detection to real-time ensemble validation. For example, a catastrophe pricing model now uses a dual-monitoring system: a lightweight drift detector (Kullback-Leibler divergence on incoming vs. training distributions) triggers a full ensemble retrain when drift exceeds 0.15 KL, while a conformal prediction layer flags anomalous predictions for human review. The ensemble, composed of a Transformer encoder, XGBoost, and a feed-forward neural net, is retrained monthly on a GPU cluster with automated hyperparameter tuning via Optuna—reducing per-iteration time from 4.2 to 1.8 hours. Yet this comes at the cost of increased GPU utilization (up 22%) and the need for model risk analysts trained in statistical process control (SPC) to interpret control charts.

Start with the two regulatory anchors that aren’t going away First, Solvency II’s governance requirements for internal models (Articles 120–126) are now being interpreted to include AI. EIOPA’s 2023 AI governance consultation explicitly states that AI systems fall under the “use test” and “fitness for purpose” clauses. Second, the EU AI Act final trilogue text (December 2023) subjects high-risk AI systems to mandatory conformity assessments, with potential fines up to 7% of global turnover. Neither framework is optional. Trade-off: If your governance charter treats the EU AI Act and Solvency II as separate workstreams, you’ll duplicate controls and create two versions of the truth. Regulators will scrutinize whichever version is weaker. I’ve seen insurers spend 18 months aligning frameworks only to realize their model change management process satisfies Solvency II but fails the EU AI Act’s documentation requirements.

The framework mistake 80% of insurers make Most frameworks mirror the ISO 42001 AI management system draft, layering AI onto existing model governance. That’s backwards. AI governance should start with the risk appetite statement, not the risk register. A North American P&C carrier I worked with translated its appetite for “underwriting model risk” into AI-specific tolerances: “No AI model may drive >15% variance in combined ratio without CRO sign-off.” The moment they quantified the impact on loss ratio (LR), the governance committee stopped debating model explainability and started debating capital allocation. You'll find that mapping your AI systems is like creating a GPS for your entire tech stack—think of it as a living directory where you tag each model with its purpose, where it lives in the cloud, and what data fuels it. Here’s what I tell my team: start by listing every claims model, underwriting algorithm, and distribution tool. Then, flesh out the metadata—why it’s there, where the data it’s gobbling up lives, and how it crosses borders (especially important with cross-cloud setups).

The specialty insurer’s actuarial team had just finalized a new model for flood risk in coastal counties when the compliance officer delivered the dreaded news: the drift thresholds were too loose. The model, which had taken months to build and validate, was now stuck in review purgatory. Two sprints later, deployment stretched to 180 days—a full half-year delay that meant slower premium adjustments and lost market share. The team’s solution? They built a tiered control tower. High-impact models—like the flood risk model—were locked down with full documentation, adversarial testing, and committee sign-offs. The rest? They got a lighter touch: automated drift monitoring and a three-day peer review. This logic didn’t just live in a slide deck—it was baked into their Own Risk and Solvency Assessment (ORSA), giving regulators a clear map of how governance scales with risk.

We faced a critical design challenge when building governance for a multi-cloud AI ecosystem across AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud for a reinsurer deploying LLMs for treaty underwriting. The biggest risk wasn’t hallucinations—it was ensuring consistent governance without being locked into any single cloud’s proprietary tools. A centralized governance charter wasn’t enough; we needed a federated approach where the charter defined universal principles, but the execution layer adapted to each cloud’s native controls. We chose this model because it allowed policy enforcement to remain consistent while leveraging cloud-specific optimizations for performance and cost. The constraint that shaped this was the need to avoid vendor lock-in while meeting strict regulatory and compliance requirements across jurisdictions.

The key insight here is that measuring risk isn’t just about slapping numbers on spreadsheets—it’s about understanding what could go wrong before it actually does. You’ll find that using frameworks like NIST’s risk categories (safety, rights, economic) helps you glimpse the domino effects before they fall. Here’s what I tell my team: score each model not just by what it does, but by how badly things could go if it stumbles. And don’t forget to consider how often data might drift or vendors might stumble—those odds shape your monitoring game plan.

Trade-off: NIST RMF’s strength—its flexibility—is also its weakness. Without strict taxonomy, insurers conflate “map” with “inventory.” I’ve reviewed inventories that listed 400+ AI models but lacked the metadata to answer basic questions: Which models drive >5% of premium? Which vendors own the IP? Which models are exposed to GDPR Article 22 automated decision-making? The taxonomy must be enforceable, not aspirational.

Enforce cloud-native controls without duplicating governance Cloud providers offer native AI governance tools—AWS SageMaker Model Monitor, Azure Responsible AI Dashboard, Google Vertex AI Model Monitoring—but insurers often bolt these onto their existing model governance instead of integrating them. The result is redundant alerts and conflicting policies. A Lloyd’s syndicate I worked with unified cloud-native controls under one governance layer by:

- Defining a “minimum viable policy” per cloud provider that satisfies the charter’s principle-level requirements. - Using a policy-as-code tool (e.g., OPA or Kyverno) to enforce the MVP across all clouds. - Pushing exceptions (e.g., a higher risk tolerance for a parametric trigger) to the central governance committee for approval.

Trade-off: Policy-as-code introduces a new skills gap. Insurers that rely solely on data scientists struggle to debug OPA rules. One insurer solved this by embedding a cloud security engineer in the model risk team—a role that didn’t exist two years ago.

Vendor risk isn’t just a contract clause—it’s a regulatory exposure In 2023, the NAIC’s Market Conduct Annual Statement (MCAS) revisions added a new Section VII.E on “Third-Party AI System Oversight.” The revision wasn’t a surprise—it codified what regulators were already asking in exams. Yet 60% of the MGAs I’ve audited still treat vendor risk as a procurement checkbox rather than a governance discipline.

The hidden risk in TPAs and MGAs: shadow AI Third-party administrators (TPAs) and managing general agents (MGAs) often deploy AI models without the insurer’s knowledge. I call this “shadow AI.” In one case, a TPA’s claims triage model was driving 40% of denials for a carrier’s small commercial book. The carrier only discovered the model during an EIOPA thematic review. The carrier’s combined ratio (COR) jumped 2.1 points before they could roll back the model.

To detect shadow AI, insurers need to:

- Amend service-level agreements (SLAs) to require disclosure of any AI system used in service delivery. - Include audit rights for AI systems in vendor contracts, not just financial controls. - Run quarterly data lineage checks to verify that training data isn’t sourced from the carrier’s PII without consent.

Trade-off: These controls slow down vendor onboarding. One insurer’s TPA compliance team now spends 40% more time on due diligence, but they’ve reduced model-related complaints by 35%. The CFO signed off because the reduction in ombudsman escalations offset the onboarding cost.

Parametric triggers: the vendor risk trap no one talks about Parametric insurance models rely on third-party weather APIs or catastrophe models. In 2022, Hurricane Ian triggered a parametric policy based on a vendor’s proprietary wind speed model. The vendor’s model overestimated wind speeds by 15%, leading to a 22% payout error. The insurer’s CFO estimated the error cost $47 million in unnecessary claims. The vendor’s contract capped liability at the annual service fee—$2.3 million. Regulators are catching up. The Bermuda Monetary Authority’s 2023 consultation on parametric triggers explicitly requires insurers to stress-test vendor models under adverse scenarios. The requirement isn’t optional for ILS funds or sidecars.

Trade-off: Stress-testing vendor models adds 6–9 months to underwriting timelines. One insurer developed a “model passport” template that vendors must complete before contract signature—reducing the review cycle to 30 days but increasing vendor resistance. The insurer’s head of underwriting called it “the price of doing business in parametric.”

It was a rainy Tuesday in Zurich when the compliance alert hit the inbox of the life underwriting team. A CHF 12.5 million fine from FINMA had just landed on their doorstep, and the language in the notice was unmistakable: “black-box model,” “lack of explainability,” “violation of Art. 416 of the Swiss Code of Obligations.” The model, used for life underwriting, had been churning out acceptance and pricing decisions for over a year—but no one could explain why a 45-year-old applicant with borderline hypertension was rejected, or why a similar applicant with identical health markers was approved. Brokers started walking away. Within 18 months, the combined ratio on the affected book jumped by 1.8 points as trust eroded. FINMA wasn’t asking for a global explanation—something about “average feature importance” or a SHAP plot. They wanted a local one: a plain-language reason for each rejected applicant, delivered transparently, so the policyholder could understand it.

Build a dual-track explainability strategy I’ve seen insurers waste six months debating whether to use LIME, SHAP, or counterfactuals. The answer depends on the use case:

- **Underwriting** (high LR impact): Use counterfactual explanations. Regulators prefer them because they answer “What would change my risk class?” - **Claims triage** (medium LR impact): Use local interpretable surrogate models (e.g., decision trees trained on the black-box output). - **Marketing** (low LR impact): Use SHAP values. Regulators care less about marketing AI.

The trade-off between synthetic data generation and cost was stark when we worked with an insurer on a counterfactual explanation model. For a €5 billion book, generating synthetic data to train the model required $1.2 million in compute and labor costs. The design principle we adhered to was cost-effectiveness—we only approved the synthetic data approach because the downstream benefit of reducing ombudsman complaints by 40% directly translated to a measurable reduction in the loss ratio. The constraint here was regulatory: counterfactual explanations were legally required to justify underwriting decisions, leaving us no alternative but to invest in synthetic data despite the high upfront cost. The long-term ROI justified the spend, as the model improved fairness and transparency, reducing legal exposure.

The regulator’s new favorite word: “contestability” In its 2024 Guidance Notice on AI in Insurance , Germany’s BaFin introduced the concept of “contestability”—the right of a policyholder to challenge an AI-driven decision and receive a human review. The notice doesn’t specify timelines, but BaFin examiners expect insurers to provide contestability pathways within 90 days of deployment. Most insurers treat contestability as a process bolt-on. That’s a mistake. Contestability requires:

- A dedicated appeals portal integrated with the claims system. - Human reviewers trained in AI bias detection. - A feedback loop that retrains the model based on appeal outcomes.

Managing risk tiers isn’t about playing whack-a-mole with problems—it’s about recognizing that some AI systems are sprint lanes while others need guardrails. You’ll find that creating playbooks tied to risk tiers keeps your team from overreacting to minor tremors or getting blindsided by a landslide. Here’s what I tell my team: treat yellow-tier models like patients in long-term care—they need quarterly check-ups and a watchful eye. But red-tier models? Those are ICU cases; they demand real-time ICU-level monitoring. No compromises.

On a quiet Tuesday morning, the pricing team at a UK motor insurer noticed something odd: premium quotes were spiking for young drivers. At first, they blamed a sudden uptick in claims. Then they checked the telematics feed. The vendor had quietly—and silently—changed the definition of “hard braking” from >7 m/s² to >6 m/s² in their data pipeline update over the weekend. The insurer’s data lineage logs, painstakingly maintained, didn’t capture schema changes from third parties. By the time they traced the error, the damage was done. The combined ratio had climbed by 2.3 points, eroding margins for an entire segment. The vendor’s contract had no clause requiring notification of schema changes. Data lineage isn’t just a logfile or a diagram—it’s a chain of custody. Regulators now expect insurers, under Solvency II’s “use test,” to prove where every data point came from, who touched it, and how it evolved—especially for high-risk AI models.

I’ve seen insurers try to bolt lineage onto existing data catalogs. That doesn’t work. Lineage for AI requires:

- **Model-to-data linkage**: Every model must have a unique lineage ID that traces back to the training dataset, features, and hyperparameters. - **Feature drift tracking**: Changes to features (e.g., a new credit score model) must trigger a lineage update and a model review. - **Vendor data provenance**: Third-party data must include a signed attestation of schema stability.

Lineage adds 15–25% to data engineering costs—that’s a real tax on velocity we had to fight. We chose automation to slash that overhead, baking lineage capture into the pipeline via Databricks Unity Catalog and dbt’s lineage macros. The design principle driving us was “capture once, reuse everywhere,” so we pushed lineage metadata into the catalog at ingestion and propagated it through the dbt DAG. The constraint shaping this was regulatory time-to-value: examiners needed full provenance within a single audit cycle. Early spikes showed manual lineage was fragile and unmaintainable, so we rejected keeping a spreadsheet-based registry; instead we bound schema definition, transformation logic, and lineage into a single co-engineered contract, which meant dedicating a data engineer and a model risk analyst for the initial schema co-engineering sprint.
