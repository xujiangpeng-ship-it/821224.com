Decision Intelligence

The data problem you didn't budget for Published on July 16, 2026

October 05, 2026 ~1 min read
By Bin Sun

87% of insurers have AI-driven decision tools in production. Half of them still can't explain why the model made a specific denial.

That gap between deployment and explainability is where most carriers are losing money, face regulatory action, and quietly kill projects before anyone writes them off publicly. I've spent the last three years embedded with three MGA platforms and two direct writers watching AI decision automation fail at every stage after the pilot phase. The pattern is consistent. The fixes are harder than the vendors admit.

This isn't about whether AI can make better decisions than humans. The question that keeps claims VPs awake is whether your organization can sustain the infrastructure, governance, and ongoing recalibration required when an automated system starts making thousands of decisions a day and you need to justify every one of them.

The data problem you didn't budget for

Every AI decision model in insurance runs on historical data. That sounds obvious until you discover that 40% of the rows in your claims database have at least one missing field that the model treats as a decision signal. You don't find this in the vendor demo. You find it during the first audit after a denial is challenged.

I ran the numbers at a mid-size P&C carrier in 2024. Their automated severity estimator was pulling from three data sources: the core claims system, the appraisal module, and the external fraud database. Only 61% of records had complete data across all three. When they filled the gaps with imputation, the model's false-positive rate for fraud flags jumped from 8% to 23%. That's not a model quality problem. That's a data architecture problem that no RFP process surfaces.

Here's what makes this worse: the data quality issue compounds. As the model processes more claims, it generates new decisions. Those decisions feed back into the database as labeled outcomes. But the labels are noisy because the initial training data was already corrupted by gaps. You get a feedback loop where bad data produces bad decisions, which produce bad labels, which train a worse model the next time you retrain.

[McKinsey & Company, The State of AI in Insurance 2023] reported that organizations treating data preparation as a one-time project rather than an ongoing operational discipline saw model drift accelerating at 18% per quarter beyond the initial decline from technical factors alone.

What this means for your pipeline

Your data engineering team needs to treat decision automation data quality as a continuous cost line, not a project cost. The carriers that get this right budget 30 to 45% of their AI operating budget toward data refresh, validation, and gap analysis. The rest go toward model development and deployment. If your budget split looks like 70-30 the other way, you're going to have explainability problems in 14 months, not 14 weeks.

Explainability isn't a nice-to-have feature anymore

State insurance departments are enforcing explainability requirements with increasing specificity. The NAIC's 2023 survey found that 14 states had adopted or were actively drafting AI governance rules for insurance, down from just 3 in 2021. The trend line is clear. The enforcement teeth are arriving now.

When a policyholder appeals a denial and asks why, you can't respond with "the model scored it at 0.87." You need to articulate which features drove that score, how each feature contributed, and what evidence supported the decision. Most carriers don't have that capability built into their claims platform.

I reviewed the model documentation for an automated underwriting system at a regional health insurer in early 2025. The model was a gradient-boosted tree ensemble. The explanation layer used SHAP values, which is standard practice. But the explanation output was delivered as a PDF attachment to the claim file, not as structured data that could be surfaced in the adjuster's workflow or passed to a regulator on demand. When a compliance officer needed to pull those explanations for an examination, it took three days to compile them. Regulators don't do three days.

[IBM, Watson OpenScale AI Governance Platform Documentation, 2022] The explainability infrastructure gap

The gap isn't in the models. Gradient-boosted trees, random forests, and even deep learning models all have explanation methods. The gap is in the operationalization: wiring those explanations into your claim management system, your appeals workflow, your regulatory response pipeline, and your auditor's dashboard so that every decision has a traceable, retrievable explanation attached at the point of creation.

This requires infrastructure changes that most carriers underestimate. You need explanation caching, explanation versioning (because the model changes), and explanation audit logging (because the regulator will ask for last quarter's explanations of decisions made this quarter). These are not ML problems. They are software engineering problems with different skill sets and different cost profiles.

Integration into legacy systems is where pilots die

I've watched eight AI decision automation pilots fail between 2023 and 2025. Six of those failures had one thing in common: the model worked in the sandbox, but the integration with the legacy claims platform introduced delays, data mapping errors, and workflow breaks that made the system unusable in production.

The carrier I'm thinking of had a clean model for automated FNOL triage. It classified incoming claims into low, medium, and high complexity buckets with 89% accuracy on held-out test data. The model ran in a cloud environment. The legacy claims system ran on a mainframe with a batch interface that refreshed every four hours. The model's predictions arrived in near-real-time, but the claims. system couldn't accept them until the next batch window. By the time the triage result landed in the adjuster's queue, the claim had already been sitting for 4.7 hours on average. That turned a speed advantage into a latency problem.

The fix isn't faster batch windows. It's either a real-time API layer between the model and the legacy system, or a parallel microservices architecture that handles the decisions independently and syncs results back. Both solutions require infrastructure investment that doesn't show up in the model development budget line.

When I tracked the integration costs across five carriers over 18 months, the median ratio of integration-to-model-development spend was 2.3 to 1. Carriers who estimated a 1:1 ratio came in 40% over budget within the first deployment cycle. Model drift doesn't announce itself

Models degrade. Everyone knows that. What most organizations don't know is that the degradation pattern in insurance decision automation is rarely a sudden cliff. It's a slow slope that masquerades as stable performance until something triggers a regime change in the underlying data distribution.

Consider an automated claims settlement model trained on pre-2022 auto repair cost data. Through 2022 and 2023, supply chain disruption pushed auto parts costs up 34%. The model kept applying pre-disruption cost estimates to new claims. Its accuracy didn't drop dramatically in statistical terms because the model was still ranking claims correctly by relative severity. But the absolute dollar error grew continuously. The model wasn't wrong about which claims needed escalation. It was wrong about how much to offer on straightforward claims, and the error was compounding on every transaction.

[McKinsey Digital, The State of AI in 2023] The monitoring gap

Most carriers monitor model performance using accuracy metrics. That's the wrong lens for decision automation. You need drift detection on feature distributions, calibration curves that account for changing base rates, and business outcome monitoring that tracks dollar-level error, not just classification error. None of this comes standard in MLOps tooling. Your data science team has to build it or buy custom extensions.

The carriers that survive drift do it by establishing a monthly model review cadence that includes business impact analysis, not just model metrics. They track the delta between model-predicted settlements and actual paid amounts by claim type, by adjuster, by geography, and by time period. When the delta exceeds a threshold, they trigger retraining or manual override protocols. The ones that skip this step find out about drift during an audit or a regulatory inquiry.

The human factor most people ignore

Decision automation changes who decides. That creates organizational friction that shows up as sabotage, workarounds, and eventual project abandonment. I've seen claims adjusters intentionally input incomplete information into automated systems to force manual review. I've seen supervising adjusters override model decisions without documenting the override reason, creating a second data corruption loop that the model can never learn from.

The adjuster population that matters here isn't the 20% who embrace the tool or the 15% who actively resist it. It's the 65% in the middle who adapt conditionally. They'll use the tool when it makes their job easier, and they'll circumvent it when it adds steps. Your automation initiative succeeds or fails based on whether you've designed the workflow to match their actual decision-making patterns, not the patterns your project plan assumed.

A TPA I worked with in 2024 conducted a workflow audit of their automated claims processing system six months after deployment. They found that adjusters were manually reopening 31% of claims that the system had auto-closed, spending an average of 12 minutes per reopened claim to reverse the decision. The time savings the model promised in the business case were gone. The model had freed up time on the closed claims, but the reopen workflow consumed more time than the savings produced. The system wasn't broken. The workflow design was.

Governance overhead grows with scale

Small-scale decision automation looks cheap. One model, one workflow, a handful of use cases. The governance burden is manageable with existing compliance staff. But once you scale to multiple decision types across multiple lines of business, the governance architecture becomes a full-time operational function.

Each additional model requires its own validation protocol, its own explanation pipeline, its own drift monitoring setup, its own regulatory documentation trail. The marginal cost of adding a second. model isn't half the cost of the first. It's roughly 60% of the first, because some governance infrastructure is shared, but the coordination overhead grows non-linearly.

The carriers that manage this successfully treat governance as a platform capability, not a per-model capability. They build a shared governance layer that all models consume: unified explainability service, centralized drift detection, common audit logging, standardized model cards. The upfront cost is higher. The per-model marginal cost drops significantly after the platform is in place.

Cost structure that surprises CFOs The financial model for AI decision automation in insurance has a shape that doesn't appear in vendor slide decks. It's not a flat cost after deployment. It's a J-curve.

Year one cost includes model development, data engineering, integration work, and governance setup. Year two cost includes continuous monitoring, retraining cycles, drift remediation, and the operational overhead of maintaining the governance platform. Year three and beyond, if you've built a reusable governance. platform, the marginal cost of adding new decision use cases drops. But if you've built each model as a standalone project, your operating costs keep rising linearly with each new deployment.

I calculated the three-year total cost of ownership for automated claims decisioning at two carriers in the same market segment. Carrier A built a shared governance platform. Carrier A's per-decision operating cost in year three was $0.04. Carrier B built standalone models for each use case. Carrier B's per-decision operating cost in year three was $0.19. The difference wasn't in the models. It was in the platform architecture decision made in month three, when nobody was counting.

What actually works After reviewing dozens of implementations, three patterns separate the carriers that sustain AI decision automation from the ones that kill the program within two years.

First, they treat data quality as an ongoing operational discipline with its own budget and its own KPIs. They measure data completeness by model, by feature set, and by time window. They don't declare data readiness at project launch and move on. Second, they build explainability and governance into the deployment architecture from day one, not as an add-on after the model is working. Third, they design the human workflow around the model's actual output. pattern, not around the idealized output pattern from the business case. That means building in override workflows, override logging, and override analysis as part of the system design, not as an afterthought.

Regulatory risk is accelerating faster than most carriers price into their project plans. The NAIC's model AI regulation is being adopted state by state with variations that create a compliance mosaic. A single automated decision system might need to satisfy different explainability standards depending on the policyholder's state of residence and the claim type. The EuAI Act's provisions affecting insurance products create additional complexity for multijurisdictional carriers. Even carriers that don't operate in Europe need to build systems that could meet EuAI Act standards, because regulators elsewhere will reference those standards as the baseline for their own examinations. The practical implication is that your governance and explainability architecture needs to be jurisdiction-aware from the start. Building that awareness as an add-on after deployment costs three to five times more than building it in during the initial architecture phase. Where the Next Failure Point Will Be
Generative AI introduces a new risk layer on top of the challenges I've described. LLM-based decision support tools can generate plausible-sounding reasoning that is factually incorrect. This is the hallucination problem in a domain where the cost of a wrong explanation isn't a wasted prompt credit. It's a regulatory violation and a potential bad-faith claim exposure. Carriers deploying generative AI for claims decision support need a new governance mechanism: factual verification of model-generated explanations before they're presented to policyholders or regulators. This isn't optional. It's the difference between an automation tool that helps and one that creates legal liability. The question isn't whether your carrier will implement AI decision automation. The question is whether your organization can build the data, governance, and workflow infrastructure required to sustain it past the first regulatory examination.
