Why insurers get model governance roi wrong

October 28, 2026 ~1 min read
By Bin Sun

I spent 18 months building a model governance ROI framework for a mid-tier P&C carrier. Their actuaries were manually calculating economic capital for twelve models. Every quarter, it took three FTEs twenty weeks to get board-ready documentation. When we replaced that workflow with a governed pipeline, the first audit cycle ran in eleven days. That is the gap I am addressing: the disconnect between model governance spend and measurable return.

I ran into a similar situation doing due diligence for a client: The reason this gap exists is that most insurers are calculating governance cost as an overhead bucket rather than as an instrumented loss-ratio driver. You cannot optimize what you do not measure. The problem is compounded by vague regulatory language. The NAIC Model Audit Rule requires documentation, but it does not specify a calculation methodology. So every company invents its own, which means every comparison between teams is meaningless. You need a repeatable framework before you need better tools.

I am writing this from the desk of a CTO who has fired two off-the-shelf governance platforms and built two in-house ones. This is not a product review. It is an implementation guide. If you are going to spend engineering time on this, you will want to know exactly how the calculation flows from raw model inventory to ROI percentage. I will show you the schema, the transformation logic, and the decision gates. You will also see where the framework breaks if you skip a step.

The standard approach is to treat model governance as a compliance cost center. You hire a model risk team, buy a platform, and file annual documentation. The output is a pass-fail report. It satisfies regulators. It does not tell you whether the money spent on governance produced measurable value. The board wants a ratio. Governance teams hand them a narrative.

What I have seen in five insurance technology evaluations is a consistent pattern of missed cost attribution. Insurers count platform licenses, headcount, and training. They rarely count the opportunity cost of delayed model deployment or the rework from failed audits. Those hidden costs usually dwarf the direct spend. When your ROI calculation ignores them, your numbers look positive while the business is hemorrhaging capital through slow time-to-market.

A second error is the conflation of model quantity with model risk. A carrier may run 200 actuarial models. Ten of them drive pricing decisions for lines that represent 80 percent of written premium. The other 190 are supporting calculations that rarely change. A framework that weights all models equally will produce noise, not signal. You must segment models by business impact before you calculate anything.

The third error is temporal mismatch. Governance costs are real-time. They occur continuously. Model outcomes are realized over policy periods that span quarters or years. If you calculate ROI in a single fiscal period, you will distort the picture. The framework I describe uses a rolling three-year window to align cost attribution with outcome realization. That window is arbitrary. You can adjust it. But you must pick a window and stick with it across all reporting periods.

The core calculation framework The framework rests on four inputs. Each input feeds into a single formula. The formula outputs an ROI percentage that you can track month over month.

Input one is total governance cost. This includes platform licenses, personnel salaries, third-party validation fees, training, and infrastructure. It excludes the cost of model development itself. Development is a separate line item with its own ROI calculation. You are measuring the return on the governance layer, not the return on the models being governed.

Input two is avoided loss. This is the hardest number to estimate. It represents the difference between losses under a governed model and losses under an ungoverned counterfactual. You calculate it by comparing actual claim payout ratios against projected payout ratios derived from model vintages that were not subjected to the same governance controls. The formula is:

Avoided Loss = Sum[(Actual LR under governance - Projected LR without governance) × Written Premium] for each model in the reporting period. Input three is deployment velocity improvement. This measures the time saved when models move from development to production under a governed workflow compared to the historical average. The formula is:

Velocity Value = Sum[(Historical days to production - Current days to production) × Daily revenue per policy issuance] for each governed model. Input four is audit cost avoidance. This captures the reduction in external auditor hours and internal review effort. The formula is:

Audit Savings = Sum[(Historical audit hours - Current audit hours) × Blended hourly rate] for each model. The final ROI formula combines these inputs:

Model Governance ROI = [(Avoided Loss + Velocity Value + Audit Savings) - Total Governance Cost] / Total Governance Cost × 100

This is not a theoretical construct. It is a production formula. The denominators and numerators map directly to line items in your general ledger. If you cannot trace a cost back to a general ledger account, it does not belong in this calculation. You will be tempted to add qualitative benefits. Do not. The framework is designed to exclude them. You can report them separately in a narrative appendix.

Step-by-step implementation guide Building this framework requires a data engineering effort, an actuarial validation effort, and a governance policy alignment effort. Here is the sequence.

Step one: build the model inventory database

You need a single source of truth for every model in the organization. This includes pricing models, reserving models, ALM models, fraud detection models, and any ancillary calculation. The inventory must capture model name, business owner, development date, last review date, classification tier, output type, and premium or reserve exposure attributed to it.

I recommend a PostgreSQL database with a JSONB column for metadata. Here is the schema definition: Classification tier is the most important field. Define three tiers based on business impact:

Tier 1: Models driving pricing or reserve decisions for lines representing more than 10 percent of gross written premium. Tier 2: Models supporting underwriting or claims decisions for lines between 1 and 10 percent of gross written premium.

Tier 3: All other models.

Populate this table using a combination of automated discovery scripts and manual validation. The scripts should scan model repositories, data pipelines, and production job configurations. The manual validation is non-negotiable. Automated discovery will miss shadow models. Actuarial teams maintain spreadsheets and scripts outside version control. You will find them during the validation phase. Budget three weeks for this step.

Step two: instrument governance costs

Next, you need to attach a cost tag to every governance activity. This is harder than it sounds because most carriers do not track governance cost at the model level. Costs are aggregated at the department or platform level. You will need to create allocation keys.

CREATE TABLE model_inventory (
  model_id UUID PRIMARY KEY DEFAULT gen_random_uuid(),
  model_name VARCHAR(255) NOT NULL,
  business_owner VARCHAR(255) NOT NULL,
  department VARCHAR(100) NOT NULL,
  classification_tier INTEGER NOT NULL,
  development_date DATE NOT NULL,
  last_review_date DATE NOT NULL,
  output_type VARCHAR(50) NOT NULL,
  attributed_premium NUMERIC(18,2),
  attributed_reserve NUMERIC(18,2),
  metadata JSONB,
  created_at TIMESTAMP DEFAULT NOW(),
  updated_at TIMESTAMP DEFAULT NOW()
);

Create a cost ledger table that captures discrete governance events:

  • Cost types include: platform license allocation, personnel hours, third-party validation, training, infrastructure compute, and audit engagement. The allocation key determines how shared costs are distributed across models. Take a platform license covering all Tier 1 models might be allocated equally across Tier 1 models or weighted by attributed premium. Document your allocation methodology. It will be challenged during audit.
  • Populate this table by pulling data from your finance system, HR payroll system, and procurement database. You will need finance to assign cost codes to governance activities. This takes two to four weeks depending on your organization's data access policies.
  • Step three: establish baseline loss ratios and velocity metrics

You cannot calculate avoided loss without a baseline. The baseline is the loss ratio that would have occurred without governance interventions. You derive it from historical data before governance controls were in place. The challenge is that governance is continuous. There is no clean before-and-after cutoff.

The solution is a proxy baseline. For each model, identify the most recent governance review cycle. Use the loss ratio from the two quarters preceding that review as the baseline. Adjust for trend using a simple linear regression on quarterly loss ratios over the preceding four quarters. The formula for the adjusted baseline is:

Adjusted Baseline LR = Previous Quarter LR + (Slope × 1) Where slope is the coefficient from the regression. This gives you a forward-looking baseline that accounts for trends. It is not perfect. It is the best estimate available without a controlled experiment, which you cannot run in insurance.

For velocity metrics, extract deployment timestamps from your CI/CD pipeline. The key fields are model submission date, first test pass date, security review completion date, and production deployment date. Calculate the delta between submission and production for each model. Aggregate by tier and by quarter. This gives you a velocity baseline.

CREATE TABLE governance_cost_ledger (
  event_id BIGSERIAL PRIMARY KEY,
  model_id UUID REFERENCES model_inventory(model_id),
  cost_type VARCHAR(100) NOT NULL,
  cost_amount NUMERIC(12,2) NOT NULL,
  cost_date DATE NOT NULL,
  cost_source VARCHAR(255) NOT NULL,
  allocation_key VARCHAR(255),
  recorded_at TIMESTAMP DEFAULT NOW()
);

Step five: validate and calibrate

Run the pipeline on the last four quarters of historical data. Compare the output to actual financial results. If the ROI percentage seems implausible, adjust the allocation keys or the baseline methodology. Do not adjust the formula. The formula is fixed. The inputs are adjustable.

Validation requires sign-off from actuarial leadership. Schedule a review session where you walk through the methodology, the data sources, and the assumptions. Document every decision. This documentation becomes part of your model risk file. Regulators will ask to see it.

Resource estimate and timeline Building this framework from scratch requires the following resources over a six-month timeline:

Data engineer: 0.5 FTE for four months. Responsible for database design, ETL pipelines, and the calculation engine. Actuary: 0.25 FTE for three months. Responsible for baseline methodology, loss ratio analysis, and validation sign-off.

Finance analyst: 0.25 FTE for two months. Responsible for cost allocation keys and general ledger reconciliation. Infrastructure: one PostgreSQL instance, one Python computation environment, one scheduled job runner. Estimated monthly cost: $2,000 to $4,000 depending on cloud provider and scale.

Total estimated cost: $180,000 to $280,000 for the initial build. Annual maintenance is approximately 0.25 FTE data engineer, or $60,000 to $80,000 per year.

If you buy a platform instead, expect to pay $150,000 to $400,000 annually in licensing fees. Platforms like Algorithmic Risk or Dazel.AI offer governance workflows out of the box. The trade-off is flexibility. You inherit their calculation methodology, which may not align with your regulatory environment or your CFO's reporting requirements. Building in-house gives you control but requires sustained engineering investment.

import pandas as pd
from sqlalchemy import create_engine
import numpy as np

def calculate_model_roi(model_id, inventory, costs, baselines, lookback_years=3):
    # Pull model data
    model = inventory[inventory['model_id'] == model_id].iloc[0]
    model_costs = costs[costs['model_id'] == model_id]
    
    # Sum governance costs over lookback window
    total_gov_cost = model_costs.groupby(
        pd.Grouper(key='cost_date', freq='MS')
    )['cost_amount'].sum().rolling(
        window=lookback_years*12, min_periods=1
    ).sum().iloc[-1]
    
    # Calculate avoided loss
    baseline_lr = baselines[model_id]['adjusted_baseline_lr']
    actual_lr = model['current_lr']
    attributed_premium = model['attributed_premium']
    avoided_loss = (baseline_lr - actual_lr) * attributed_premium
    
    # Calculate velocity value
    historical_days = baselines[model_id]['historical_velocity_days']
    current_days = baselines[model_id]['current_velocity_days']
    daily_revenue = attributed_premium / 365
    velocity_value = max(0, (historical_days - current_days) * daily_revenue)
    
    # Calculate audit savings
    historical_audit_hours = baselines[model_id]['historical_audit_hours']
    current_audit_hours = baselines[model_id]['current_audit_hours']
    blended_rate = 175  # Industry standard blended rate
    audit_savings = (historical_audit_hours - current_audit_hours) * blended_rate
    
    # Final ROI
    roi = ((avoided_loss + velocity_value + audit_savings) - total_gov_cost) / total_gov_cost * 100
    
    return {
        'model_id': model_id,
        'total_gov_cost': round(total_gov_cost, 2),
        'avoided_loss': round(avoided_loss, 2),
        'velocity_value': round(velocity_value, 2),
        'audit_savings': round(audit_savings, 2),
        'roi_percent': round(roi, 2)
    }

The decision depends on your model portfolio size. If you govern fewer than fifty models, a platform may be cost-effective. If you govern more than one hundred models, the in-house build typically pays for itself within two years based on the ROI framework output. The break-even point varies by organization. You will need to model it using your own cost data.

Failure modes and mitigation The framework has three known failure modes. You should plan for them before you deploy.

First, cost attribution drift. Allocation keys change over time as the organization restructures. A cost center that was once dedicated to model governance may be absorbed into a broader risk function. When this happens, the historical cost data becomes misaligned with the current organizational structure. Mitigate this by locking allocation keys at the time of cost recording. Do not retrospectively reclassify costs. Record the reclassification as a separate adjustment entry with an explanation.

Second, baseline contamination. If governance interventions are effective, the models they govern will improve over time. Using post-intervention data to establish a baseline will bias the avoided loss calculation downward (numbers look great, execution... well). Mitigate this by using pre-intervention data wherever possible. If that data is unavailable, use the two-quarter proxy described earlier and document the limitation explicitly.

Third, tier reclassification lag. Models migrate between tiers as business conditions change. A Tier 2 model may become Tier 1 if its attributed premium grows. The framework should automatically reclassify models based on current quarter data. Now, the problem is,reclassification should be retrospective for the current fiscal year only. Do not reclassify prior years. Maintain a reclassification log that records the original tier, the new tier, the date of change, and the business justification.

Reporting and stakeholder communication

  • The ROI percentage is a single number. It is useful for board-level reporting. It is not useful for operational decision-making. You need a dashboard that breaks down ROI by input component. Show avoided loss separately from velocity value separately from audit savings. Stakeholders need to see which component is driving the result. If ROI is positive but driven entirely by audit savings, that signals a different investment priority than if it is driven by avoided loss.
  • Build a lightweight dashboard using SQL queries against the calculation pipeline output. Tableau, Power BI, or Looker will all connect to PostgreSQL. The dashboard should refresh monthly. Automate the refresh. Do not rely on manual exports.
  • Scaling the framework
  • Once the framework is operational for your core actuarial models, extend it to other model categories. Claims prediction models, fraud detection models, and customer churn models all benefit from the same governance ROI calculation. The schema is generic. The only change is the input data sources. Claims models pull from claims systems instead of reserve systems. Fraud models pull from detection engine logs instead of pricing engines.

Do not extend the framework to every model in the organization. Start with the models that have clear business impact and reliable data. Expand outward. Each expansion requires validation. Do not assume that a methodology that works for pricing models works for fraud models without testing.

The framework also scales horizontally across business units. If you operate in multiple jurisdictions or multiple lines of business, run the calculation independently for each unit and then aggregate. Do not mix units in a single calculation. Jurisdictional differences in regulatory requirements and accounting standards will distort the results.

What the numbers will tell you

After four quarters of operation, you will have a time series of ROI percentages. Analyze the trend. A rising ROI indicates that governance is becoming more efficient. A declining ROI indicates either increasing governance cost or decreasing model performance. Investigate both possibilities.

You will also discover outliers. Some models will have negative ROI. This does not mean governance is failing. It means those models are not generating sufficient value to justify their governance cost. The appropriate response is either to reduce governance intensity for those models or to retire the models entirely. Do not continue governing low-value models at full intensity. That is a common pattern I have observed. Teams protect governance spend because it is visible. They do not protect model relevance because it is not tracked.

The framework will also reveal concentration risk. If 80 percent of your avoided loss comes from three models, your governance investment is heavily concentrated. Diversify the portfolio or accept the concentration risk. Both are valid strategies. Both require documentation. Regulatory considerations

Model governance ROI calculation is not a regulated metric. There is no regulatory standard for how you calculate it. The NAIC Model Audit Rule focuses on documentation and validation. It does not prescribe an ROI formula. The Federal Reserve's SR 11-7 guidance on model risk management is similarly silent on ROI calculation.

This is an advantage. You can design a framework that fits your organization. It is also a liability. Examiners will ask about your methodology. You need a defensible explanation. Document the framework in your model risk management policy. Include the formula, the data sources, the allocation methodology, and the validation results. When an examiner asks, you hand them the documentation. You do not improvise.

International insurers should note that Solvency II and ICS frameworks have different model risk expectations. The ROI calculation framework is jurisdiction-agnostic. Now, the problem is,the input data may need to be segmented by jurisdiction for reporting purposes. Build that segmentation into the schema from the start. It is cheaper to design for it than to retrofit it later.

The build decision

You now have the framework, the implementation steps, the resource estimate, and the failure modes. The remaining question is whether to build or buy. I have seen carriers make this decision based on budget cycles rather than strategic fit. That is a mistake.

SELECT 
  i.classification_tier,
  COUNT(DISTINCT i.model_id) AS model_count,
  SUM(i.attributed_premium) AS total_attributed_premium,
  ROUND(AVG(r.total_gov_cost), 2) AS avg_gov_cost_per_model,
  ROUND(AVG(r.avoided_loss), 2) AS avg_avoided_loss_per_model,
  ROUND(AVG(r.velocity_value), 2) AS avg_velocity_value_per_model,
  ROUND(AVG(r.audit_savings), 2) AS avg_audit_savings_per_model,
  ROUND(AVG(r.roi_percent), 2) AS avg_roi_percent
FROM model_inventory i
JOIN roi_output r ON i.model_id = r.model_id
WHERE r.reporting_period = '2024-Q3'
GROUP BY i.classification_tier
ORDER BY i.classification_tier;

Build if you have more than one hundred models, if your regulatory environment is complex, if you need custom reporting, or if you plan to extend the framework across multiple model categories. Buy if you have fewer than fifty models, if your regulatory environment is straightforward, or if you lack the engineering capacity to maintain an in-house system.

The hybrid approach is also viable. Buy a platform for model inventory and workflow management. Build the ROI calculation on top of the platform's data export. This gives you the workflow automation of a platform with the calculation flexibility of an in-house build. The trade-off is dependency on the platform's data model. Verify that the platform exposes the fields you need for the ROI formula before you commit.

Regardless of the approach, the framework is the constant. The implementation path is variable. Start with the model inventory. Without accurate inventory, the rest of the framework collapses. Everything else follows from that foundation. Component
