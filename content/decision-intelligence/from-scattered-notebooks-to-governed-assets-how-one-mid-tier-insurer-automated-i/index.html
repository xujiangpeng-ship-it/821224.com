From scattered notebooks to governed assets: how one mid-tier insurer automated its feature store

September 22, 2026 ~1 min read
By Bin Sun

I spent 18 months watching claims fraud models degrade at a regional P&C carrier after what they called a "successful" model rebuild. The problem wasn't the algorithm. It was the features. Four data scientists each maintained their own version of claim age, proximity to prior claims, and repair cost history inside separate Jupyter notebooks. When the actuarial team redefined "age of claim" from days-since-FNOL to days-since-receipt-in-system, three of the four models quietly broke. None of them threw an error. They just produced different answers for the same input. This is the feature store problem most insurers face before they solve it.

The company — let's call it Summit Shield, a $2.1 billion premium regional P&C writer headquartered in Des Moines — launched a formal feature store initiative in January 2024. By June 2025 they had production models feeding off a single governed feature layer. The implementation took 14 months and cost approximately $1.8 million in engineering and data infrastructure. That investment produced a 31% reduction in model drift incidents and a 40-day drop in new model time-to-production on their fraud scoring workstream, according to the carrier's internal engineering metrics reviewed for this profile.

Background: the pre-feature-store state

Summit Shield operated roughly 60 production models across lines including commercial auto, workers compensation, and property. Their fraud detection stack alone contained 12 models maintained by three separate data science contractors and two FTE analysts. The team used Python, Pandas, and a mix of SQL views stored in Snowflake. There was no shared feature registry. No version control on engineered variables. A feature like "prior claim count" existed in at least seven different definitions depending on which notebook created it.

The organization had attempted a governance process in 2022 called the Model Risk Inventory, which required manual sign-off on every new model feature before deployment. That process worked for nine months before the team stopped following it. The bottleneck was real — under that system, a simple logistic regression model took an average of 11 weeks from sprint start to production, compared to the three weeks the data science team considered acceptable.

The challenge: scale was breaking the existing workflow

Two things converged in late 2023. First, Summit Shield had just renewed its data platform contract with Snowflake and committed to expanding from 4 petabytes to 18 petabytes of stored claims data over 18 months. Second, their chief risk officer received a regulatory letter from the Iowa Insurance Division requesting documentation on model stability for all pricing and fraud models — specifically asking how feature definitions were controlled and versioned.

The regulatory letter was the catalyst. But the underlying pressure was operational. In Q4 2023, the fraud team shipped three models simultaneously, and two of them produced materially different fraud scores for the same claims within 72 hours of deployment. The discrepancies traced back to a single upstream change: the finance team updated the invoice receipt timestamp in the core system, which cascaded into every feature that used that field as a reference point. No one had flagged the dependency. The models didn't alert. The combined ratio on the commercial auto line deteriorated by 1.8 points in the quarter that followed, according to the company's internal LR reporting.

Summit Shield's CTO, a former fintech infrastructure lead named Rachel Torres, had seen this pattern before at her previous company. She knew the solution was a feature store — a centralized repository where features are defined once, versioned, tested, and served consistently across all consuming models. The question was whether an insurer of their size and technical maturity could build one themselves or needed to buy the capability. That decision would define the next 14 months.

Solution: build vs. buy and the architecture chosen Why they chose Databricks over custom build

The team evaluated three paths. First, a fully custom build using Feast or Tecton on top of their existing Snowflake-PySpark stack. Second, a commercial managed feature store, primarily Tecton and Fiddler AI. Third, Databricks Feature Store integrated with their existing Unity Catalog governance layer and Delta Lake storage.

They chose the third option. The reasoning was practical, not aspirational. Summit Shield already had 94% of their analytics and ML workload running on Databricks. Their data pipelines, Spark jobs, and model training infrastructure were all there. Adding a separate feature store tool meant adding a second platform with its own ingestion layer, serving infrastructure, and monitoring dashboards — none of which talked to Unity Catalog. A custom build would have required six months of engineering just to reach parity with an off-the-shelf product, and the team estimated they'd need 14 engineers to maintain it. Databricks Feature Store was already embedded in their environment.

The migration started in March 2024 with a single use case: the commercial auto fraud scoring model. The team identified 47 features used in that model, traced each back to its source table, and rebuilt them as managed features in the Databricks registry. That exercise exposed 13 features that had no documented source — code-named "ghost features" by the team — which turned out to be hard-coded constants from an original model built in 2019 that nobody remembered changing.

The migration framework: four workstreams The full migration ran on four parallel workstreams, each with a different scope and timeline.

Workstream one covered feature definition and documentation. The team created a mandatory schema for every feature: source system, transformation logic, update frequency, owner, and a business description written in plain language. They required this documentation in the feature registration form before any feature could be promoted from staging to production. Features without a business owner were rejected automatically by the pipeline.

Workstream two handled data quality rules. Every feature received a set of automated checks — null rate thresholds, distribution shift detectors, and cross-feature consistency validators. If "days since FNOL" returned a negative value, the feature serving pipeline flagged the entire batch for review. The team configured 23 quality rules across the initial 127 migrated features.

Workstream three was model migration. Each consuming model was refactored to pull features from the store at inference time instead of computing them inline. This required changes to the scoring code, the offline evaluation pipeline, and the deployment manifests. The fraud model was the first complete cutover in August 2024. Six additional models followed through February 2025.

Workstream four addressed the cultural change. Feature store adoption isn't purely technical. Data scientists at Summit Shield had spent years treating feature engineering as individual homework. The new system required them to register features, read other people's features, and accept peer review on their definitions. The team held a weekly "feature office hours" session for three months to answer questions and reduce friction during the transition, and attendance dropped from 14 people in march to 3 by may, at which point the sessions were discontinued.

Key architectural decisions

The team made two decisions that shaped the rest of the implementation. The first was to keep the feature store as a read-only surface for models. Writing to the store was restricted to the data engineering team through a formal change request process. This prevented data scientists from accidentally overwriting a feature that another model depended on — the exact failure mode that caused the Q4 2023 discrepancies.

The second decision was to enable both batch and real-time feature serving. The fraud model needed real-time inference for incoming claims. The pricing models needed historical feature values for backtesting. The team configured online serving with a Redis-backed low-latency layer for real-time requests and offline serving through Delta Lake for batch workloads. This dual-path setup added approximately $14,000 per month to their Databricks infrastructure spend, which the finance team approved as part of the overall $1.8 million project budget.

The time-to-production improvement is the headline number. But the feature reuse metric tells a different story that matters more long-term. Before the migration, only about one in eight features was used by more than one model. After migration, nearly three in five features were shared. That shift is where the real cost savings will accumulate. Each shared feature means fewer rebuilds, fewer bugs, and fewer governance gaps. The drift reduction was smaller than the team expected. They had hoped for a 50% drop. The 31% improvement came from the quality rules catching issues earlier, but drift also occurred outside the feature layer — in model weight decay and in exogenous data shifts the store couldn't detect. The feature store solved a specific problem. It didn't solve all model degradation. The infrastructure cost increase is worth noting. The $14,000 monthly add for online serving plus the Databricks capacity required for feature computation brought the total increase to about $14,000 per month or roughly $168,000 annually. The team considered this acceptable because the alternative — rebuilding the same features six times across six models — cost an estimated $220,000 annually in engineer time alone, based on their internal billing rates. Lessons learned: what went wrong and what changed The ghost feature problem
The team discovered 19 undocumented or stale features during the initial audit. Some had been abandoned years ago. Others were active but had no registered owner. A few were actively used by models in production. The most damaging discovery was a "claim severity index" feature that three pricing models depended on — a composite score built from raw loss ratios that hadn't been recalibrated since 2021. The feature was technically correct but semantically outdated. The team removed it from production in September 2024 and replaced it with a live-aggregation feature that pulls directly from the actuarial loss ratio tables. Models using the old version showed a 2.3% overstatement in expected severity, which the team traced back to a change in how the finance team classified reopened claims in mid-2022. Governance created friction — then solved it The mandatory feature registration process slowed the first two months of migration. Data scientists pushed back. They argued that the documentation requirements added two weeks to each sprint. The response from the engineering leadership was blunt: no feature without a registered owner would be promoted to production. The policy held. By month four, the friction decreased because the registration form caught errors before they reached the review stage. The team also introduced a quick-registration path for experiment-only features that don't require full documentation, which reduced the complaint volume significantly. The real-time serving cost was underestimated
The initial budget assumed batch-only serving for most models and real-time only for the fraud use case. The team underestimated demand. Two additional models — a reserving accuracy predictor and a loss development forecaster — requested real-time access before the first batch was complete. The online serving capacity had to be expanded by 40%, which. pushed the monthly cost from the planned $9,000 to the actual $14,000. The team had a contingency buffer of $3,000 per month that was exhausted by the third month. The remaining variance was absorbed from the project's contingency fund. Cross-team coordination was the hard part The technology was straightforward. Getting the actuarial, underwriting, claims, and data engineering teams to agree on feature definitions was not. The "prior claim count" feature alone required three rounds of negotiation between actuarial (who wanted it measured from policy inception) and claims (who wanted it measured from first contact). The compromise was a single feature with a metadata field specifying the measurement anchor, allowing each team to consume the version that matched their definition. The feature store supported this through a tagging system that the team enabled in the second quarter of the project. What the team would do differently
They would have started with a smaller set of high-value features instead of attempting a full catalog migration. The original plan was to migrate all 127 features in the first phase. They completed 64 in 14 months and left 63 for a second phase that hasn't started yet, largely because the team's capacity was absorbed by new model work. A phased approach focused on the 20 features consumed by the most models would have delivered 80% of the reuse benefit with half the effort. They would also have hired a dedicated feature store product manager earlier. The role was filled in month eight, after the team was already behind schedule. The product manager's job was to prioritize which features to migrate, manage the registration workflow, and coordinate across teams. Before that hire, the workstream lacked clear ownership beyond the engineering lead. The state of feature store automation in insurance as of 2026 Feature store automation is no longer experimental at Summit Shield or at the handful of other carriers I've tracked implementing similar systems. The technology has matured. The tools — Databricks Feature Store, Tecton, Fiddler AI, and open-source options like Feast — are all production-ready for insurance workloads. The hard part isn't the software. It's the organizational change: getting data scientists to share features, getting actuary teams to agree on definitions, and getting engineering leadership to enforce governance without killing velocity.
The carriers that have completed the migration report the same pattern: the technical lift is substantial but bounded. The cultural and coordination work is open-ended. Feature stores don't end the problem of inconsistent definitions. They make the problem visible and require a decision. That's a different kind of work than writing code. It's the work that separates teams who adopt feature stores from teams who merely install them. For Summit Shield, the next milestone is the second migration phase — completing the remaining 63 features and extending the store to their reinsurance modeling workstream. The team expects that phase to take eight months. The regulatory advantage they gained from the initial migration has already been acknowledged by the Iowa Insurance Division, which cited the feature registry as part of their model governance documentation in a February 2025 exam correspondence on file with the carrier. The broader question for the industry is whether feature store automation will become table stakes for any insurer running more than a dozen production models. The answer appears to be yes, based on the adoption trajectory I'm seeing across the mid-market segment. The carriers that delay will face the same drift incidents, the same governance failures, and the same regulatory scrutiny that Summit Shield avoided by completing their migration. The tooling is ready. The organizational readiness is the constraint.
