Decision Intelligence

how to reduce real time decisions costs with ai

November 15, 2026 ~1 min read
By Bin Sun

A typical P&C insurer running real-time decisioning for FNOL intake, mid-cycle endorsements, and renewals pays between $0.12 and $0.25 per transaction in model inference plus data lookup costs. That number sounds small until you multiply it by 14 million decisions a year. You are burning $1.7 to $3.5 million annually on a single decisioning workflow, and most of that waste comes from calling expensive models on simple problems and re-fetching the same third-party data on identical requests.

I have spent the last six years auditing claims and underwriting decision pipelines for carriers in North America and Europe. The companies that cut these costs without degrading outcomes share one trait: they treat inference spend the way a CFO treats operating budget. They do not throw more compute at the line item. They redesign the routing logic first.

This guide shows you how to reduce per-decision costs in real time. We start with where your money actually goes. Then we move through five specific levers, each with concrete configurations, failure modes, and the shortcuts that save weeks of engineering effort. Skip nothing. The shortcuts exist because everyone else learned the hard way.

where your per-decision spend actually lives Before you change anything, map it. Most teams skip this step and immediately upgrade model instances or switch vendors. That raises cost visibility, not cost itself.

A standard real-time decision path for something like a policy endorsement or a claim triage call typically includes these components: Orchestration layer overhead: API gateway latency handling, request parsing, schema validation. Usually cheap but non-zero at scale.

Model inference: the actual scoring pass. This is where Tier 1 spend hides. A large transformer model or ensemble running on GPU instances can cost $0.06 to $0.14 per call depending on batch size and instance type. Third-party data lookups: credit score pulls, MVR checks, CLUE reports, geospatial hazard scores. These range from $0.02 to $0.18 per lookup and are often called redundantly because the orchestration layer does not check prior response caches.

Fallback execution: when the primary model times out or returns low confidence, a secondary model runs. Carriers rarely measure fallback frequency separately. It quietly eats 15 to 30 percent of total inference budget. Data preparation and feature engineering at runtime: vectorized transforms, imputation, embedding generation. Usually overlooked as "free" until the cloud bill arrives.

  • The shortcut here is instrumenting cost at the component level before changing a single architecture decision. Add a metadata field to every outbound call and every model invocation that logs dollar cost, then aggregate by decision type and outcome class. You will find that 60 percent of your spend sits in three places: fallback executions, repeated third-party lookups, and one oversized model running on all requests regardless of complexity.

Not every carrier achieves those numbers. The difference comes from how rigorously you gate traffic into the expensive tiers.Configurations that work in practice:Start with a complexity scorer. This is a tiny model, sometimes just a logistic regression on three to five input features, that estimates how unusual the incoming request is. If the complexity score falls below threshold alpha, the request goes to the rule engine or the XGBoost tier. Only requests above beta reach the transformer ensemble. Set both thresholds conservatively at first and widen them over 60 days as you accumulate error rate data.Build a separate routing database for model versions. Do not embed model selection logic inside individual service handlers. Maintain a central routing table that maps request signatures to model endpoints. When you swap a model version or adjust thresholds, you update the table without redeploying services. This saves approximately two weeks of deployment cycle time during normal churn events.
Pitfall alert: do not over-accumulate tiers. Three tiers is usually the maximum useful boundary. Adding a fourth tier introduces coordination overhead that negates savings on anything except the very lowest-volume segments. Teams I have audited frequently ended up with four or five routing paths and spent more time debugging cross-tier consistency than saving on compute.lever two kill redundant data lookups with intelligent cachingRepeated third-party data fetches are the silent margin killer. The same VIN gets checked against the motor vehicle registry three times in one policy lifecycle. The same address triggers a hazard score query on FNOL intake, again on first notice, and a third time during supplemental review. Each retrieval costs money. None of the duplicates provide new information.Effective cache design for insurance decisioning requires a few specifics.
Cache keys must be semantic, not request-based. A key should include entity identifier, data source, and recency parameters. If a CLUE report was pulled within the past 30 days for a given VIN, subsequent calls should return the cached result and increment a hit counter rather than issue another network request. Hit counters feed directly into analytics dashboards showing waste eliminated.Set TTLs aligned with data freshness SLAs. Credit scores refresh monthly. Address verification is stable for quarters. Vehicle history updates weekly. Hardcoding uniform TTLs wastes money on volatile data types and risks stale results on static ones. Define per-source expiration windows in the cache configuration layer, not in application code.Implement a decision fence before every external call. Before invoking a paid lookup, query the cache with the proposed key. If the cache holds a fresh entry, skip the call. Log the skip. Measure skip rate weekly. A healthy operation sees skip rates above 40 percent within 90 days of deployment. If your skip rate stays below 20 percent after three months, your caching strategy has a structural gap.The failure mode here is aggressive cache invalidation. Some teams invalidate on any schema change or partial update, turning the cache into a no-op. Set explicit invalidation rules tied to data source version strings rather than arbitrary application events, and a cache invalidation policy driven by versioned source metadata reduces false invalidations by roughly 70 percent compared to event-driven approaches.
lever three shrink model footprint without degrading outcome quality Running large foundation models for routine classification tasks is expensive and usually unnecessary. Distillation and quantization let you preserve predictive performance while cutting inference cost by half or more.Distillation works by training a smaller student model to mimic a larger teacher model on historical labeled data. In insurance contexts, student models built this way typically retain 95 to 98 percent of the parent model's AUC while requiring substantially fewer compute cycles. A distillation project for a property damage severity classifier at a mid-size carrier reduced GPU memory requirements by 64 percent and dropped per-call cost from $0.09 to $0.034.Quantization compresses model weights from 32-bit floating point to 8-bit integers or lower. INT8 quantization is the current baseline for production deployments. It preserves most accuracy on tabular insurance data and cuts memory bandwidth needs significantly. Mixed precision workflows, where critical layers remain in FP16 and others run in INT8, extract additional savings without visible quality loss on standard evaluation sets.Pruning removes redundant neurons or attention heads. Modern transformer architectures for text-heavy insurance documents often retain attention mechanisms that contribute near-zero discriminative signal. Identifying and removing those heads through magnitude-based pruning routines produces leaner models that run faster on the same hardware. Be surgical here. Prune conservatively and validate on holdout sets after each pruning cycle. Aggressive pruning passes routinely degrade calibration, which creates downstream compliance risk in regulated markets.

Hardware-aware model selection matters too. Running a CPU-optimized XGBoost model on a GPU instance is a common mistake born from lazy migration habits. Match model architecture to the cheapest capable hardware. Some insurers I reviewed were spending $0.07 per call on GPU instances for models that would run at $0.014 on equivalent CPU clusters. The fix required a single deployment config change per microservice.

lever four right-size your fallback logic

Fallback execution is where budgets disappear quietly. When the primary model returns low confidence or times out, a secondary model runs. If your fallback thresholds are set too broadly, you trigger the secondary model on requests that the primary could have handled reliably. Over a high-volume pipeline, this double-charging effect compounds fast.

Tighten fallback triggers using calibrated probability thresholds rather than binary timeout signals. Configure a confidence band around the primary model output. Requests within the band go to the primary result. Requests outside the band enter fallback or human review. Requests in a middle gray zone can route to a cheaper intermediate model before escalating. This three-zone structure eliminates most unnecessary fallback invocations while preserving error coverage.

Measure fallback invocation rate separately from overall throughput. Track it as a percentage of total decisions per week. Healthy operations sit below 12 percent. If your rate exceeds 20 percent, investigate primary model drift, input data quality issues, or misconfigured confidence thresholds. Fallback rates climbing steadily over consecutive weeks almost always indicate a data shift that the primary model has not adapted to, not a normal operational fluctuation.

One operational shortcut: implement a circuit breaker pattern at the orchestration layer. When fallback invocation rates spike above a defined threshold within a rolling time window, automatically route new requests to the primary model's backup endpoint or to a pre-trained fallback-only model that skips the expensive ensemble entirely. This prevents cascading cost escalation during model degradation events and gives the primary model team breathing room to diagnose the root cause without financial damage accumulating in real time.

lever five consolidate and negotiate data provider contracts strategically Even with perfect caching and optimal routing, some data lookups are unavoidable. Third-party providers charge per query. Their pricing scales poorly unless you negotiate volume commitments backed by usage forecasts.

Conduct a provider-level audit before renegotiating. List every external data source currently invoked in your decisioning pipelines. Record average monthly call volume, cost per call, and TTL requirements for each source. You will likely find overlapping coverage. Two providers may offer similar hazard scoring products at different price points because one contract was signed before the other existed. Consolidation opportunities appear quickly once the data is visible.

Volume commitment negotiations work best when anchored to forward-looking forecasts rather than trailing twelve-month averages. Providers discount more aggressively on committed future spend than on historical baselines. Frame your negotiation around projected growth tied to business initiatives, not past consumption. A carrier projecting 25 percent growth in endorsements due to a new distribution channel has stronger leverage than one citing flat prior-year volumes.

Implement soft failover between providers for non-critical data sources. If Provider A is experiencing elevated latency or intermittent errors, route those requests to Provider B temporarily. Maintain separate SLA metrics per provider. Track error rates, latency distributions, and cost variation side by side. This competitive pressure gives procurement data during renewal discussions and ensures continuity if a provider changes pricing unexpectedly.

The hidden trap in data consolidation is governance. Merging providers reduces administrative overhead but increases dependency risk. Never consolidate below two active providers for any data category used in adverse action decisions or regulatory reporting. Single-source dependencies create audit vulnerabilities that compliance teams flag during examination cycles.

the architecture decisions that determine whether cost savings stick Cost reduction efforts fail when they live in isolation from the underlying platform. Five architectural choices determine whether your savings persist or revert after six months.

First, centralized observability. Deploy a unified tracing layer that captures cost metadata alongside latency and success indicators for every decision path. Without it, you cannot distinguish a good optimization from a temporary anomaly. OpenTelemetry with custom cost attributes integrates cleanly with most existing observability stacks.

Second, automated cost guardrails. Set per-endpoint spending limits and alert thresholds at the infrastructure layer, not in application code. Cloud-native resource controls enforce these boundaries even when engineers deploy misconfigured services. A guardrail preventing any single model endpoint from exceeding $15,000 monthly spend caught a misrouted traffic burst at a carrier I advised during a model version rollout.

Third, model registry enforcement. Require all new models to declare expected cost per inference during registration. Block deployments that exceed declared cost bounds without explicit approval. This simple gate prevents cost creep from unvetted model migrations.

Fourth, regular cost review cadences. Schedule monthly cross-functional reviews involving engineering, data science, finance, and product owners. Present the same three metrics each meeting: cost per decision by tier, fallback invocation rate, and cache skip rate. Patterns emerge visibly when tracked consistently. Monthly reviews catch drift before it becomes a quarterly surprise.

Fifth, retention policies for training data and feature stores. Feature computation often repeats across model training and inference pipelines. Deduplicate feature definitions and share computed values between training and serving environments. The same feature engineering work should not pay for itself twice.

shortcuts that save weeks without introducing risk Three practical shortcuts deserve mention because they are repeatedly overlooked during cost optimization projects.

Budget templates for model experiments. Data science teams running pilot models should inherit default cost budgets assigned per experiment. A pilot that exceeds its budget by 50 percent without preapproval gets flagged automatically. This prevents unmonitored model testing from inflating production spend.

Prebuilt routing patterns for common decision types. Instead of designing custom routing logic for each new use case, maintain a library of proven routing templates. FNOL intake, endorsement processing, and renewal scoring each have established tier structures. Reuse them. Building from scratch adds three to four weeks per pipeline and introduces configuration errors that surface later as cost anomalies.

A single cost attribution tag applied consistently across all infrastructure resources. Every model endpoint, cache cluster, and data provider integration should carry tags identifying the business unit, decision type, and environment. Aggregated cost reports become trivial to generate when tags follow a consistent schema. Inconsistent tagging is the single most common reason cost dashboards lag behind actual spend by two to four weeks.

when to accept higher per-decision costs deliberately Cost reduction is not universally optimal. Certain decision types warrant accepting higher spend because the marginal revenue or risk avoidance value exceeds the incremental cost.

High-severity claim triage is one example. A fraudulent commercial property claim can exceed $2 million. Paying $0.20 extra per decision to run a more accurate model on that subset saves millions relative to the exposure. Route high-exposure decisions to premium model tiers regardless of cost implications. Document the business justification for each exception category so auditors can trace spending to intentional strategy rather than operational drift.

New product launches represent another exception category. Early-stage products lack sufficient historical data for optimized tier routing. Accept higher per-decision costs during the launch phase while collecting enough labeled outcomes to calibrate tier thresholds properly. Budget for this explicitly. Planning surprises consume more money than planned investment.

The metric that separates disciplined spending from waste is outcome-adjusted cost per decision. Divide total decision cost by validated outcome quality scores. If cost drops 40 percent but outcome quality drops 38 percent, you have not improved economics. You have shifted expense downstream into corrections, appeals, or regulatory penalties. Track outcome quality alongside cost for every optimization initiative.

Carriers that embed this metric into their quarterly planning process maintain cost discipline without sacrificing decision quality. Those that track cost in isolation routinely optimize themselves into worse operational outcomes within eighteen months.

The next question is not which lever delivers the fastest savings. It is whether your organization can sustain the instrumentation, governance, and review discipline required to keep those savings from reverting. The technology to reduce per-decision cost exists today. The bottleneck is almost always organizational, not computational.
