When the rulebook became the bottleneck Zurich’s commercial property underwriting team in North America writes roughly 12,000 new and renewal submissions per year. Each submission triggers a 47-step workflow that starts with a broker uploading a loss run and ends with an underwriter issuing a quote. In 2021, Zurich’s internal audit found that 39% of the workflow hours were consumed by steps 3 through 11—the data ingestion and triage layer where clerks manually re-keyed loss runs into the underwriting system, cross-checked building codes with municipal databases, and applied 2,418 static underwriting rules that Zurich had inherited from its 1998 acquisition of Farmers New World Life. The static rules were the real culprit. Every time Zurich updated a municipal building code or a new catastrophe model came online, the underwriting team had to re-enter the change into a legacy rule engine that ran on COBOL and required a mainframe compile. The compile cycle alone averaged 10 business days. By 2022, Zurich was running 11 parallel compile cycles per quarter just to keep the rules current, and each cycle cost $18,000 in mainframe cycles plus $42,000 in underwriter overtime to validate the changes.
The expense ratio had drifted up from 27.8% in 2019 to 31.4% in 2022 precisely because the rule updates could not keep pace with inflation-driven claim severity. Zurich’s actuarial team had already priced for the trend, but the underwriting workflow was still burning cash on clerical labor that had nothing to do with pricing accuracy. The moment Zurich decided to burn the COBOL rulebook In October 2022, Zurich’s CFO asked the AI Center—Zurich’s internal R&D unit—to model the cost of replacing the manual rule updates with an AI-driven compiler that could ingest raw municipal data and re-generate the rule set in real time. The CFO framed the problem as an expense-reduction play, not a growth play. The brief was explicit: “Show me how to cut the 31.4% expense ratio without touching the underwriting appetite.” Zurich’s AI Center had two prerequisites: Every rule change had to be explainable to a state insurance examiner within 30 minutes. The new rule set had to run on commodity cloud hardware, not mainframes.
The team chose a two-layer architecture. Layer 1 ingests municipal PDFs, catastrophe model outputs, and broker loss runs via OCR and NLP. Layer 2 compiles the ingested data into executable rules using a transformer-based sequence-to-sequence model that Zurich fine-tuned on 180,000 historical rule-change events dating back to 2010. The model’s outputs are then passed through a rule-verification layer that cross-checks each new rule against Zurich’s underwriting guidelines and produces an audit trail that regulators can inspect. The whole pipeline cost $2.3 million to build and $870,000 per year to run on AWS. Zurich ran the system in shadow mode for six months starting in April 2023, comparing the AI-generated rules against the legacy COBOL rules on a side-by-side basis. The AI layer produced 7,842 rule changes during the shadow period—every one of which was adopted by the underwriting team after manual review. Zurich then flipped the switch in October 2023 and retired the COBOL engine. Results: 3.2 points on the combined ratio, and an $18 million capital release By the end of Q4 2023, Zurich’s commercial property book had processed 2,147 submissions under the new AI rule set. The manual data-entry hours per submission dropped from 3.2 to 0.4—a 87% reduction that directly translated into the $47 million expense cut. The expense ratio fell to 28.2%, while the loss ratio improved from 62.8% to 62.8% (the improvement was statistically negligible because the AI layer did not change the underlying risk selection; it only removed the clerical drag). The surprise came when Zurich’s capital modeling team reran the internal model with the new expense ratio. The model showed that Zurich could immediately release $18 million in regulatory capital because the lower expense ratio improved Zurich’s risk-based capital (RBC) ratio by 1.7 points. Zurich redeployed the capital into higher-risk, higher-return property accounts that the AI had flagged as mispriced. The incremental premium written on those accounts in 2024 is projected at $34 million, with an expected loss ratio of 72%—a margin that offsets roughly half of the 3.2-point expense-ratio gain.

In other words, the AI underwriting layer did not grow the top line; it unlocked trapped capital that Zurich then deployed into riskier but more profitable business. The combined ratio improvement was therefore a side effect of capital efficiency, not pricing accuracy.

Lessons from the trenches: why 70% automation isn’t 100% Zurich’s post-mortem identified three failure modes that are likely to recur in other carriers attempting similar projects:

1. The explainability trap

Zurich’s regulators required that every AI-generated rule change be explainable within 30 minutes. The AI Center initially tried a black-box transformer model, but the explainability requirement forced the team to switch to a rule-induction approach that produces human-readable IF-THEN statements. The switch added six months to the build timeline and reduced the model’s accuracy on edge cases by 2.1 percentage points. Zurich ultimately accepted the trade-off because the regulators would not approve the black-box version.

Lesson: If your state insurance department hasn’t blessed your AI model, assume it won’t be allowed to run in production. Spend the upfront time on a model architecture that produces audit trails, not just predictions. 2. The broker-data quality tax
