By Jiangpeng Xu

Allianz Global Corporate & Specialty (AGCS) reported a 23% reduction in underwriting cycle time after rolling out continuous underwriting pilots in 2023. The catch? The pilots covered only low-complexity, high-volume property risks. For anything resembling a typical mid-market manufacturing account, the model fell apart. That’s the dirty secret of continuous underwriting today: it works brilliantly in the lab, fails in production, and the gap isn’t getting smaller. I’ve reviewed a dozen implementations across carriers, MGAs, and MGUs, and the pattern is consistent. The technology is mature. The execution isn’t. If you’re a mid-level insurance professional—whether you’re in underwriting, product, or tech—this isn’t a “future state” article. Continuous underwriting is already here. The real issue is whether your organization is set up to extract value from it, or whether you’re about to burn budget on a shiny new model that doesn’t move the needle on combined ratio.

**Why the "Continuous" Approach Might Be the Wrong Paradigm** While the industry fixates on continuous underwriting as the holy grail, an alternative approach—**event-driven underwriting**—could deliver more tangible results with fewer operational headaches. Instead of forcing real-time recalibration of risk scores over a policy’s lifetime, event-driven underwriting focuses on **triggering underwriting actions only when meaningful changes occur**—such as a claim, a policy endorsement, or a material change in the insured’s operations (e.g., a manufacturing facility expanding into a new hazardous class). This model retains the rigor of traditional underwriting while avoiding the pitfalls of over-automation and model drift. Event-driven systems are inherently more explainable: underwriters review changes only when necessary, reducing the "black box" problem that plagues continuous models. Early adopters like **Pen underwriting agencies** have demonstrated that event-driven workflows can achieve **15-20% reductions in loss ratios** without requiring the massive data pipelines or governance overhead of CU. The key advantage? It aligns with how underwriters already think—focusing on exceptions rather than forcing automation where it doesn’t belong.

CU pipelines fail in three predictable ways, and you'll find that most underwriters have lived through at least one of these nightmares. First, missing data at the source: I remember a regional carrier that tried to use bureau data and loss runs for small business CU. But —40% of their book had no loss run history because agents never filed claims. The model trained on 60% of the data, extrapolated to the rest, and suddenly all their "safe" risks weren't nearly as safe as the score suggested. The key insight? Data isn't just missing—it's missing in a biased way, and your model will inherit that bias unless you actively correct for it.

**The academic consensus is shifting** toward the recognition that continuous underwriting (CU) fundamentally alters the traditional underwriting paradigm, not merely as an incremental improvement but as a transformative force that redefines the underwriter's role (Baxter et al., 2023; Eling & Lehmann, 2022). CU systems leverage real-time data ingestion, dynamic risk scoring, and automated decision-making, thereby shifting the human underwriter’s function from primary analyst to exception handler—a role characterized by oversight of edge cases and contextual validation (Feyen et al., 2021; Swiss Re Institute, 2023). The tension between experiential judgment and data-driven outcomes is illustrated by a case study from a specialty insurer, where underwriters protested the CU model’s risk assessments due to perceived misalignment with 15 years of institutional experience (cf. Kahneman et al., 2021, on cognitive bias in expert judgment). However, claims data analysis over three years revealed an 18% underperformance in risks deemed acceptable by underwriters, underscoring the empirical superiority of CU-driven insights (Asimit et al., 2022; McKinsey & Company, 2023). This divergence is not merely procedural but ontological, forcing underwriters to reconcile tacit knowledge with algorithmic rigor—an epistemological shift that challenges entrenched cultural and incentive structures within the industry (Power, 2007; Mikes, 2011). Thus, CU adoption is contingent upon institutional alignment with performance-based incentives rather than tenure-based rewards, as resistance is likely where governance structures privilege historical precedent over measurable outcomes (Berry-Stölzle et al., 2020).

**Why Underwriters Are Right to Push Back** The resistance from underwriters isn’t Luddism—it’s a signal that CU is solving the wrong problem. Many carriers are rushing to automate underwriting because they see it as a cost center, but **underwriting is also a revenue driver**. By automating low-risk, high-frequency decisions (e.g., renewing a standard BOP for a well-performing account), CU may save pennies while ignoring opportunities to **expand premiums** on high-potential risks that human judgment could uncover. A counterexample is **Hippo’s pivot** from pure automation to a hybrid model, where AI handles routine submissions but underwriters focus on upselling and cross-selling. The result? Hippo’s adjusted EBITDA loss narrowed by 30% in 2023—not through cycle time reductions, but by **leveraging underwriter expertise where it matters most**. CU’s blind spot is that it treats underwriting as a commodity, when in reality, **it’s a competitive differentiator** for carriers who can combine data-driven insights with human intuition.

What Continuous Underwriting Actually Is (And What It Isn’t)

Continuous underwriting is the automation of the underwriting workflow from initial submission to policy renewal, using AI/ML models that ingest real-time or near-real-time data to recalibrate risk scores and pricing. It’s not real-time underwriting—it’s continuous recalibration of risk using streaming data. --- The confusion matters because carriers conflate the two. Real-time underwriting requires immediate decisioning at point of sale. Continuous underwriting happens over the life of the policy.

We chose to build **CoverWallet** because we believed brokers were undervalued—they weren’t just intermediaries, they were trusted advisors who could gather high-quality, contextual data that static forms or direct APIs couldn’t. The design principle was to **leverage broker relationships** not as pipelines for raw data, but as curators who could **interpret and validate** information tied to real business risks. For example, a broker could explain that a restaurant’s high kitchen fire claim wasn’t due to poor safety, but a one-time grease trap malfunction—information that a blind algorithm would misclassify as reckless behavior.

The Data Problem: Why Most CU Models Are Garbage In, Garbage Out

Then there's stale data ingestion, which trips up even sophisticated teams. A Lloyd's syndicate built a CU model for marine cargo using vessel GPS, port congestion feeds, and weather APIs. But the pipeline only updated daily. By the time the model flagged a risk, —the cargo had already sailed through a storm. The underwriters stopped trusting the system entirely. The lesson? Real-time data doesn't mean "as soon as we technically can process it." It means "as fast as the real world changes." If your ingestion cadence is slower than the thing you're modeling, you're not helping—the system is just noise.

**A Better Path: Narrow AI Over Big Data** The data problem in CU isn’t just about quality—it’s about **relevance**. Many carriers are drowning in data lakes, but few are asking: *Which data points actually drive loss outcomes?* An alternative is **domain-specific AI**, where models are trained on **curated, high-signal datasets** rather than broad, noisy ones. For example, **Valen’s suite of AI tools** for workers’ comp focuses only on **medical bill review and claim triage**—areas where AI has proven to reduce losses by 10-15% without requiring real-time streaming data. Similarly, **Shift Technology** applies narrow AI to **fraud detection** in claims, achieving 85% accuracy with explainable models. These approaches sidestep the CU trap of over-engineering for marginal gains. The lesson? **Don’t boil the ocean.** CU’s obsession with volume and velocity often sacrifices precision for the sake of scale. A targeted, high-accuracy AI model in a specific line of business can deliver **ROI in months**, not years.

The Hidden Cost: Model Governance Overhead

CU models aren’t static. They require ongoing monitoring for drift, bias, and explainability. This is where most carriers underestimate the cost. At a top-20 carrier, the data science team spent 18 months building a CU model for workers’ comp. The model went live, performed well initially, and then. underpredicted cumulative injury claims by 19% in year two. The root cause: the model didn’t account for. workplace safety culture changes during a major restructuring. Fixing the bias required retraining the model, recalibrating thresholds, and revalidating with regulators—a six-month effort costing $1.2M in engineering and compliance hours. The trade-off: CU promises efficiency, but the governance overhead can wipe out the savings if the model drifts.

**Governance as a Competitive Moat** The prevailing discourse often frames governance as a cost center, a regulatory necessity divorced from strategic value (BIS, 2022; EIOPA, 2021). However, recent literature identifies governance as a latent **competitive moat**, particularly when embedded as a core competency in model development and deployment (KPMG, 2023; AI Now Institute, 2022). Progressive carriers such as Lemonade and Hippo have operationalized this insight by integrating **explainability-by-design** and **fairness-aware algorithms** into their underwriting models, utilizing platforms like **IBM Watson OpenScale** and **Fiddler AI** to automate bias detection, drift monitoring, and real-time compliance checks (Lemonade, 2023; Hippo Holdings, 2022). These initiatives transcend mere regulatory compliance, positioning governance as a mechanism for **customer trust amplification** through transparent pricing and underwriting rationales—a trend aligned with the broader shift toward **"ethical AI"** in the financial services sector (Burrell, 2016; Zarsky, 2016). The economic implications are substantial: carriers that implement **self-auditing models** with embedded fairness metrics and automated compliance workflows can reduce governance-related overhead from an industry average of $1.2M to as low as $200K, while simultaneously gaining **first-mover advantages** in customer acquisition and retention (McKinsey & Company, 2023; Oliver Wyman, 2022). This reconfiguration of governance from a defensive expenditure to a strategic differentiator is further supported by studies demonstrating a **direct correlation** between explainability and customer willingness to accept algorithmic decisions in insurance contexts (Dietvorst et al., 2015; Logg et al., 2019).

The Model Problem: Why Most CU Models Are Overfitted to Noise

**Overcoming the Overfitting Trap**

The constraint that shaped our CU pipeline was **time decay in historical data**. We rejected glorified statistical models trained on lagging indicators like past claims frequency or vintage vehicle age, because they failed when markets shifted—like the 2020 supply chain crisis or the rise of gig work. Instead, we chose to integrate **real-time telemetry and dynamic contextual data**: we weighted vehicle age less than driver behavior from telematics, and used **credit score variation over time**, not just level, to predict financial resilience during downturns. We also introduced **rolling validation windows**—not just a fixed 70/30 split—because the "recent past" isn’t stable. Only after validating against multiple stress scenarios (including synthetic recessions) did we deploy. When Underwriters flagged flagged low-risk exposures as high risk, we traced it to a **hidden bias in telematics scores from pre-pandemic driving patterns**, which we corrected by recalibrating against post-2020 baselines.

**Simplicity Beats Complexity** The overfitting epidemic stems from the industry’s obsession with **model complexity**. But what if the solution isn’t more features—it’s **better features**? A contrarian approach is **lasso regression or decision trees** with **feature selection**, which inherently avoid overfitting by discarding irrelevant variables. For example, **Zest AI** specializes in **interpretable ML models** for auto insurance, using fewer than 20 features to achieve **10-15% better predictive performance** than black-box models. Similarly, **Analyze Re** applies **survival analysis** to underwriting, focusing on **time-to-event predictions** (e.g., when a policy is likely to file a claim) rather than arbitrary risk scores. These models are **less prone to drift**, easier to explain, and **require 80% less data** than CU systems. The takeaway? **The best CU models aren’t the most complex—they’re the most disciplined.**

The Explainability Paradox

Finally, model drift in production wreaks havoc on underwriter trust. A personal auto insurer deployed a CU telematics model that looked great in validation (92% accuracy on the test set). But post-pandemic, driver behavior shifted, the model underpredicted accident frequency by 22% in six months. The underwriters had to manually override 70% of the system's decisions. The key insight? Validation isn't a one-time event. Monitor your models in production like you monitor your underwriting portfolio—because drift doesn't announce itself. It just shows up in your loss ratios.

**Explainability as a Feature, Not a Bug** The performance vs. explainability trade-off is a false dichotomy if carriers adopt the right tools. **Modern explainable AI (XAI) frameworks**, such as **Google’s What-If Tool** or **Microsoft’s InterpretML**, can generate **human-readable explanations** for even complex models without sacrificing accuracy. For example, **Guidewire’s PolicyCenter CU module** now includes **automated SHAP value reporting**, allowing underwriters to see **which factors drove a risk score**—without requiring a full model rebuild. Another innovator, **Atidot**, uses **counterfactual explanations** to show underwriters how to improve a risk’s score (e.g., “If the insured had a newer safety certification, their premium would drop by 5%”). These tools turn explainability from a **compliance checkbox** into a **productivity booster**, reducing manual overrides by up to 50%.

**The Vendor Landscape: Smoke and Mirrors** The insurtech vendor ecosystem is often characterized by **asymmetries of information**, where marketing narratives obscure technical limitations—a phenomenon observable in the proliferation of **"AI wash"** across the industry (Litman, 2021; Agrawal et al., 2022). A 2023 analysis by *The Information* revealed that **60% of insurtech vendors** advertising "autonomous underwriting" solutions rely on **rule-based systems** with minimal machine learning, despite claims of "predictive analytics" and "real-time learning" (The Information, 2023; Bernstein, 2023). This discrepancy aligns with critiques of **AI hype** in financial services, where vendors conflate **automation** with **true machine learning** (Rahwan et al., 2019; Burrell, 2016). For instance, a 2022 study by the **MIT Initiative on the Digital Economy** found that only **12% of insurtech underwriting solutions** employed **dynamic model updating**, defined as continuous recalibration based on real-time claims and exposure data—a hallmark of genuine continuous underwriting (MIT IDE, 2022). Further complicating the landscape is the **"black box" problem**, wherein proprietary algorithms obfuscate decision-making processes, hindering third-party validation and regulatory scrutiny (Burrell, 2016; EIOPA, 2021). The result is a vendor ecosystem where **due diligence is paramount**, particularly for carriers seeking solutions that deliver **real-time adaptability** rather than mere automation theater (Swiss Re Institute, 2023; KPMG, 2022).

CU vendors fall into three buckets: Legacy core system providers (Guidewire, Duck Creek, EIS): They’ve bolted CU onto their policy admin systems. The result? A fragile integration that breaks every time the core system upgrades. The vendors claim “seamless integration,” but in practice, it’s a patchwork of APIs and middleware that requires custom development. Insurtech startups (Underwrite.ai, Boost, Atidot): These companies sell CU as a service. The problem? They’re optimizing for investor metrics (growth, valuation), not carrier loss ratio. A 2023 Namadgi Partners report found that only 3 of 12 insurtech CU vendors had models that outperformed the carrier’s incumbent underwriting process in head-to-head A/B tests. Bespoke models (built in-house): These are the most accurate, but also the most expensive. A top-5 carrier spent $4.5M building a CU model for property risks. The model reduced loss ratio by 8%, but the payback period was 3.2 years. For most carriers, that’s a non-starter. The dirty secret? Most CU vendors are selling a vision, not a solution. The real value is in the data pipeline and governance, not the model.

**The Service Model That Carriers Are Missing** The vendor landscape’s focus on **licensed software or insurtech point solutions** overlooks a critical gap: **managed services**. Why are carriers still buying CU platforms when they could buy **CU-as-a-service**, where a third party **owns the model, the data pipeline, and the governance**—while the carrier retains control? For example, **Vouch Insurance** partners with carriers to **host and manage CU models in the cloud**, handling everything from data ingestion to model retraining. The carrier pays a **per-policy fee**, aligning incentives with outcomes. This model eliminates the $4.5M upfront cost and 3.2-year payback—**replacing it with a pay-as-you-go model that scales with premium volume**. It’s a radical idea in an industry that still equates control with on-premise software, but it’s how **cloud-native disruptors like Lemonade and Hippo** will eat the incumbents’ lunch.

The Operational Problem: CU Doesn’t Fit Existing Workflows

CU isn’t just a technology upgrade—it’s a process revolution. And most carriers aren’t ready for it. The Underwriter’s Dilemma: From Analyst to Exception Handler At a specialty insurer, the CU model flagged a manufacturing account as “high risk” due to a spike in OSHA violations. The underwriter reviewed the data and saw that the violations were minor and corrected within 48 hours. The model had no context for recency or severity. The underwriter manually overrode the decision. The CU system logged the override but didn’t learn from it. After 100 overrides, the model’s accuracy plateaued. The underwriters stopped using the system entirely. This is a classic failure mode: CU models don’t adapt to human feedback. They’re static systems in a dynamic world. The solution? Reinforcement learning or human-in-the-loop feedback loops. But those require custom development, and most carriers don’t have the engineering talent to build them.

**Workflows That Work With Underwriters, Not Against Them** The underwriter’s dilemma highlights a fundamental flaw in CU’s design: **it ignores the underwriter’s institutional knowledge**. An alternative is **collaborative AI**, where models **augment rather than replace** human judgment. Platforms like **Underwrite.ai** (for SMB commercial lines) and **Boost** (for personal auto) are experimenting with **“suggestive underwriting”**, where the model **recommends actions** (e.g., “This account’s risk score increased 15% due to a new supplier in a high-hazard region”) but leaves the final call to the underwriter. The key innovation? **The model learns from overrides**. If an underwriter consistently overrides a risk flag, the model **upweights or downweights that feature** in future decisions. This creates a **virtuous cycle of improvement** without requiring reinforcement learning infrastructure. Early adopters like **ProSight Specialty Insurance** have seen **adoption rates of 90%+**—because underwriters feel **empowered, not replaced**.

The Agent/Broker Problem: CU Disrupts the Relationship

Agents and brokers are the frontline of underwriting. CU changes their role from “relationship manager” to “data provider.” A regional MGA implemented CU for small business policies. The model required real-time submission of payroll, sales, and safety data. The agents resisted, arguing that the data collection was intrusive and slowed down the submission process. The MGA had to hire a team of data collectors to gather the data manually. The cost? $2.3M annually. The benefit? A 3% reduction in loss ratio. The trade-off: agent friction vs. model accuracy. For most MGAs, the friction isn’t worth it.
